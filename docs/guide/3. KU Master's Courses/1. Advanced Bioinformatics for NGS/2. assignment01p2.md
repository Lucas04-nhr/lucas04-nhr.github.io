## Likelihood Model {#likelihood-model}

::: note
For site $i$:

- $i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$ indexes genomic sites, where $N=4397$.
- $D_i$ is the sequencing depth at site $i$.
- $j \in \{1,\ldots,D_i\}$ indexes reads covering site $i$.
- $b_{ij} \in \{A,C,G,T\}$ is the observed base in read $j$ at site $i$.
- $Q_{ij}$ is the Phred quality score associated with $b_{ij}$.
- $\epsilon_{ij}$ is the probability that the observed base is incorrect.
- $Z_i \in \{A,C,G,T\}$ is the unobserved true allele at site $i$.
- $\pi_z$ is the genomic frequency of allele $z$, where $z \in \{A,C,G,T\}$.
:::

The parameters to estimate are therefore

$$
\boldsymbol{\pi}
=
(\pi_A,\pi_C,\pi_G,\pi_T),
$$

subject to

$$
\pi_A+\pi_C+\pi_G+\pi_T=1.
$$

Because the organism is haploid, each site has exactly one underlying allele even when different bases are observed among the reads.

### Sequencing Error Model {#sequencing-error-model}

The supplied quality strings use Phred offset 33. For an ASCII quality character $c_{ij}$,

$$
Q_{ij}
=
\operatorname{ASCII}(c_{ij})-33.
$$

The corresponding sequencing error probability is

$$
\epsilon_{ij}
=
10^{-Q_{ij}/10}.
$$

I assume that, conditional on an error occurring, each of the other three nucleotides is equally likely. Therefore,

$$
P(b_{ij}\mid Z_i=z,Q_{ij})=
\begin{cases}
1-\epsilon_{ij}, & b_{ij}=z,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{\epsilon_{ij}}{3}, & b_{ij}\neq z.
\end{cases}
$$

::: info
A high-quality base contributes strong evidence for one allele, whereas a low-quality mismatch is not treated as equally informative. This is important because the quality scores in the data vary substantially.
:::

### Likelihood for One Site {#site-likelihood}

Assuming the reads covering a site are conditionally independent given the true allele,

$$
P(X_i\mid Z_i=z)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{D_i}
P(b_{ij}\mid Z_i=z,Q_{ij}),
$$

where

$$
X_i
=
\{(b_{ij},Q_{ij})\}_{j=1}^{D_i}
$$

denotes all observed sequencing information at site $i$.

Since $Z_i$ is not observed, it is marginalised out using the law of total probability:

$$
P(X_i\mid\boldsymbol{\pi})
=
\sum_{z\in\{A,C,G,T\}}
P(X_i\mid Z_i=z)P(Z_i=z\mid\boldsymbol{\pi}).
$$

Because

$$
P(Z_i=z\mid\boldsymbol{\pi})=\pi_z,
$$

the single-site likelihood becomes

$$
P(X_i\mid\boldsymbol{\pi})
=
\sum_{z\in\{A,C,G,T\}}
\pi_z
\prod_{j=1}^{D_i}
P(b_{ij}\mid Z_i=z,Q_{ij})
$$

### Likelihood Across All Sites {#full-likelihood}

Assuming independence between genomic sites,

$$
L(\boldsymbol{\pi})
=
P(X\mid\boldsymbol{\pi})
=
\prod_{i=1}^{N}
P(X_i\mid\boldsymbol{\pi}),
$$

so that

$$
L(\boldsymbol{\pi})
=
\prod_{i=1}^{N}
\left[
\sum_{z\in\{A,C,G,T\}}
\pi_z
\prod_{j=1}^{D_i}
P(b_{ij}\mid Z_i=z,Q_{ij})
\right]
$$

and the log likelihood is

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\pi})
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log
\left[
\sum_{z\in\{A,C,G,T\}}
\pi_z P(X_i\mid Z_i=z)
\right].
$$

The calculation was implemented on the log scale to avoid numerical underflow.

## EM Algorithm {#em-algorithm}

The unobserved allele $Z_i$ is treated as a latent variable. The EM algorithm alternates between calculating its posterior distribution and updating the allele-frequency parameters.

### E-step {#e-step}

At EM iteration $t$, define

$$
w_{iz}^{(t)}
=
P(Z_i=z\mid X_i,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(t)}).
$$

Using Bayes' theorem,

$$
w_{iz}^{(t)}
=
\frac{
\pi_z^{(t)}P(X_i\mid Z_i=z)
}{
\displaystyle
\sum_{k\in\{A,C,G,T\}}
\pi_k^{(t)}P(X_i\mid Z_i=k)
}
$$

for every site $i$ and candidate allele $z$.

The value $w_{iz}^{(t)}$ can be interpreted as the posterior probability that site $i$ truly contains allele $z$.

The expected complete-data log likelihood is

$$
Q(\boldsymbol{\pi}\mid\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(t)})
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{z\in\{A,C,G,T\}}
w_{iz}^{(t)}
\log
\left[
\pi_z P(X_i\mid Z_i=z)
\right].
$$

Expanding the logarithm,

$$
Q
=
\sum_i\sum_z
w_{iz}^{(t)}\log\pi_z
+
\sum_i\sum_z
w_{iz}^{(t)}
\log P(X_i\mid Z_i=z).
$$

The second term does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\pi}$, so the M-step only needs to maximise

$$
\sum_i\sum_z
w_{iz}^{(t)}\log\pi_z
$$

subject to

$$
\sum_z\pi_z=1.
$$

### M-step {#m-step}

Maximising the expected complete-data log likelihood gives

$$
\pi_z^{(t+1)}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
w_{iz}^{(t)}.
$$

Thus, each new allele frequency is the average posterior probability of that allele across all sites.

The algorithm was initialised with equal allele frequencies,

$$
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(0)}
=
(0.25,0.25,0.25,0.25),
$$

and iterations were continued until

$$
\max_z
\left|
\pi_z^{(t+1)}-\pi_z^{(t)}
\right|
<
10^{-10}.
$$

The algorithm converged after ==**7 iterations**==.

## Implementation {#implementation}

The pileup file was parsed in Python. Each ASCII quality character was first converted to its Phred score and then to an error probability:

```python
Q = ord(quality_char) - 33
epsilon = 10 ** (-Q / 10)
```

The conditional probability of an observed base was implemented as

```python
if observed_base == true_allele:
    p = 1.0 - epsilon
else:
    p = epsilon / 3.0
```

For numerical stability, site likelihoods were stored on the log scale and marginal likelihoods were evaluated using a log-sum-exp calculation.

The essential EM updates were:

```python
# E-step
log_numerator = np.log(freqs) + site_log_lik[i]
weights[i] = np.exp(
    log_numerator - logsumexp(log_numerator)
)

# M-step
new_freqs = np.mean(weights, axis=0)
```

## AIC Model Comparison {#aic-model-comparison}

The Akaike Information Criterion is

$$
AIC
=
-2\log L+2K,
$$

where $K$ is the number of free parameters.

### Fixed Model {#fixed-model-aic}

For the fixed model,

$$
K_{\mathrm{fixed}}=0
$$

because all four allele frequencies are specified in advance.

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
AIC_{\mathrm{fixed}}
&=
-2(-7749.9138995)+2(0)\\
&=
15499.8277990.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Estimated Model {#estimated-model-aic}

The estimated model contains four allele frequencies, but they must sum to one. Therefore, only three parameters are free:

$$
K_{\mathrm{MLE}}=3.
$$

Hence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
AIC_{\mathrm{MLE}}
&=
-2(-7746.6514893)+2(3)\\
&=
15499.3029786.
\end{aligned}
$$

::: table title="AIC Comparison" align="center" copy="all"
| Model | Log likelihood | Free parameters $K$ | AIC |
| :---- | -------------: | --------------------: | ---: |
| Fixed $(0.33,0.17,0.17,0.33)$ | -7749.9138995 | 0 | 15499.8277990 |
| Estimated by EM | -7746.6514893 | 3 | **15499.3029786** |
:::

The AIC difference is

$$
\Delta AIC
=
15499.8277990
-
15499.3029786
=
\boxed{0.5248205}.
$$

The EM-estimated model therefore has the lower AIC and is preferred according to this criterion. However, the difference is only approximately **0.52 AIC units**, which is very small.

::: note
The improvement in log likelihood is

$$
-7746.6514893-(-7749.9138995)
=
3.2624102.
$$

This improves $-2\log L$ by about $6.52$, but estimating three additional free parameters incurs an AIC penalty of $2\times3=6$. Therefore, only a small AIC advantage remains for the estimated model.
:::

Consequently, the data do not provide strong evidence that the freely estimated allele frequencies fit substantially better than the fixed frequencies once model complexity is taken into account.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

A maximum-likelihood model incorporating read-specific sequencing error probabilities was used to estimate nucleotide composition from low-depth sequencing data of a haploid fission yeast genome.

The estimated allele contents were

$$
(\hat\pi_A,\hat\pi_C,\hat\pi_G,\hat\pi_T)
=
(0.34477,0.17417,0.16587,0.31520)
$$

with an estimated GC content of

$$
\widehat{GC}=0.34003
$$

or approximately **34.00%**.

The maximum-likelihood model achieved a log likelihood of $-7746.6515$, compared with $-7749.9139$ under the fixed allele frequencies. After penalising the three additional free parameters, the estimated model had an AIC of $15499.3030$, compared with $15499.8278$ for the fixed model.

Thus, the estimated model is preferred by AIC, but only by approximately **0.52 units**, indicating that the two models receive very similar support from the data.
