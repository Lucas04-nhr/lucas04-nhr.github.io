## Methods and conventions {#methods}

Analytical times are measured in **generations before the present**. Empirical plots use **years before the present**, with mutation rate $\mu=2.5\times10^{-8}$ per base per generation and generation time $g=25$ years. The teaching model also uses recombination rate $r=10^{-8}$ per base per generation. These assumed rates set the scale of the results; they are not estimated by the analytical exercise.

The epoch order is **E3, E2, E1**, from recent to ancient. In Question 2, each epoch is represented by its conditional mean coalescence time. This is the assignment's reset approximation, rather than the complete PSMC transition kernel.

For Question 3, the three male samples are:

::: table align="center" title="Samples used for the human PSMC analyses"

| Sample  | Population |                        Description                         |
| :-----: | :--------: | :--------------------------------------------------------: |
| NA18486 |    YRI     |                 Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria                  |
| NA06984 |    CEU     | Utah residents with Northern and Western European ancestry |
| NA18530 |    CHB     |               Han Chinese in Beijing, China                |

:::

Each population is represented by **one individual**. The curves are descriptive estimates from those individuals and chromosomes; they are not replicated population-level estimates or measurements of census population size.

## Coalescence in three epochs {#question-1}

### Epoch-specific coalescence rates {#coalescence-rates}

For two lineages in a diploid population of effective size $N_k$, the per-generation coalescence rate is

$$
\lambda_k=\frac{1}{2N_k}.
$$

::: table align="center" title="Demographic epochs and pairwise coalescence rates"

|     Epoch      | Time interval, generations ago | Effective size $N_k$ | Rate $\lambda_k$ per generation |
| :------------: | :----------------------------: | :------------------: | :-----------------------------: |
|   E3: recent   |           $[0,2000)$           |        20,000        |       $2.5\times10^{-5}$        |
| E2: bottleneck |         $[2000,2500)$          |        2,000         |       $2.5\times10^{-4}$        |
|  E1: ancient   |        $[2500,\infty)$         |        10,000        |        $5\times10^{-5}$         |

:::

The bottleneck has the largest instantaneous rate: ten times the recent rate and five times the ancient rate. Its short duration still matters when calculating the probability of an event occurring there.

### Probability of coalescing in each epoch {#epoch-probabilities}

For an exponential waiting time, survival over $L$ generations has probability $e^{-\lambda L}$. Coalescence in E2 requires surviving E3 first, and coalescence in E1 requires surviving both finite epochs:

$$
P_3=1-e^{-2000/(2N_3)}=1-e^{-0.05}=0.0487705755,
$$

$$
P_2=e^{-0.05}\left(1-e^{-500/(2N_2)}\right)
=e^{-0.05}(1-e^{-0.125})=0.1117724037,
$$

$$
P_1=e^{-0.05}e^{-0.125}=e^{-0.175}=0.8394570208.
$$

The three events are mutually exclusive and cover all possible coalescence times. E1 extends indefinitely with a positive coalescence rate, so a pair surviving to E1 eventually coalesces with probability one:

$$
P_1+P_2+P_3=1.
$$

Thus $P_1>P_2>P_3$. Approximately 84% of pairs coalesce in the ancient epoch, despite the bottleneck having the greatest rate. **A high instantaneous rate is different from a high total probability over an epoch.**

### Conditional expected coalescence times {#conditional-times}

The requested $\dot t_k$ is the absolute time conditional on coalescence occurring in epoch $k$. For $W\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ conditioned on $W<L$,

$$
E[W\mid W<L]
=\frac{\int_0^L w\lambda e^{-\lambda w}\,dw}{1-e^{-\lambda L}}
=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{L}{e^{\lambda L}-1}.
$$

For an epoch beginning at time $a$, add $a$ to this conditional waiting time. Consequently,

$$
\dot t_3=40000-\frac{2000}{e^{0.05}-1}=991.6670139,
$$

$$
\dot t_2=2000+4000-\frac{500}{e^{0.125}-1}=2244.7930225.
$$

After surviving to 2500 generations, the residual waiting time in E1 has mean $2N_1=20000$ generations:

$$
\dot t_1=2500+20000=22500.
$$

::: table align="center" title="Conditional absolute coalescence times"

| Epoch | $\dot t_k$, generations ago |
| :---: | :-------------------------: |
|  E3   |          991.6670           |
|  E2   |          2244.7930          |
|  E1   |         22500.0000          |

:::

The finite-epoch means lie within their respective intervals. Using $2N_k$ alone for E3 or E2 would ignore truncation and would not answer the conditional-time question.

## A PSMC-light hidden Markov model {#question-2}

### Hidden states and population-size dependence {#hmm-definition}

Let $S_x$ be the epoch containing the local coalescence time at base $x$, and let $O_x$ equal 1 for a heterozygous base and 0 for a homozygous base. The three hidden states are E3, E2, and E1.

Population sizes determine the coalescence hazards, epoch probabilities, and conditional mean times. Those times determine the emission and transition probabilities. If population sizes were to be estimated, these quantities could be recomputed for candidate sizes and their Forward likelihood compared. Here the supplied sizes are used to evaluate and decode the model; no parameter optimization was performed.

### Emission probabilities {#hmm-emissions}

Two lineages with coalescence time $\dot t_k$ have total branch length $2\dot t_k$. Under the teaching approximation, at least one mutation produces a heterozygous observation:

$$
h_k=P(O_x=1\mid S_x=k)=1-e^{-2\mu\dot t_k},
$$

$$
P(O_x=0\mid S_x=k)=e^{-2\mu\dot t_k}.
$$

::: table align="center" title="Emission probabilities in E3, E2, E1 order"

| State | Representative time | Heterozygous emission | Homozygous emission |
| :---: | :-----------------: | :-------------------: | :-----------------: |
|  E3   |      991.6670       |   0.00004958212146    |  0.99995041787854   |
|  E2   |      2244.7930      |   0.00011223335249    |  0.99988776664751   |
|  E1   |     22500.0000      |   0.00112436742474    |  0.99887563257526   |

:::

Older genealogies have more opportunities for mutation and higher expected heterozygosity. The expression neglects recurrent and back mutations and replaces all within-epoch coalescence-time variation with one representative value.

### Initial distribution and recombination transitions {#hmm-transitions}

The requested initial and redraw distribution is

$$
\pi=(P_3,P_2,P_1)
=(0.0487705755,\ 0.1117724037,\ 0.8394570208).
$$

Conditional on time $T$ generations, the probability of no recombination over $d$ bases is $e^{-2rTd}$. Without recombination, the state remains unchanged. Following recombination, the assignment redraws the epoch independently from $\pi$; this redraw can return to the same epoch.

Define $q_l(d)=e^{-2r\dot t_l d}$. Then

$$
A_{lk}(d)=q_l(d)\mathbf{1}_{\{l=k\}}+(1-q_l(d))\pi_k.
$$

In E3, E2, E1 order,

$$
A(d)=\begin{pmatrix}
q_3+(1-q_3)\pi_3 &(1-q_3)\pi_2 &(1-q_3)\pi_1\\
(1-q_2)\pi_3 &q_2+(1-q_2)\pi_2 &(1-q_2)\pi_1\\
(1-q_1)\pi_3 &(1-q_1)\pi_2 &q_1+(1-q_1)\pi_1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each row sums to one. For successive bases,

$$
A(1)\approx\begin{pmatrix}
0.999981134130&0.000002216798&0.000016649072\\
0.000002189548&0.999960123153&0.000037687299\\
0.000021941822&0.000050286266&0.999927771912
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

::: note Consistent units and the reset approximation
With a common reference $N_0$, conventional scaled quantities are $\rho=4N_0r$ and $t=T/(2N_0)$, so $\rho t=2rT$. The exponent uses $2r\dot t_l d$ because the conditional times here are in generations. For $N_0=10000$, the per-base reference-scaled rates are $\rho=0.0004$ and $\theta=0.001$.

Although the assignment describes $\pi$ as stationary, the stated reset kernel has state-dependent departure rates and generally does not satisfy $\pi A=\pi$. I use $\pi$ as the prescribed initial and redraw distribution. Likewise, the arbitrary-distance expression is a one-reset approximation, not generally an exact continuous-time semigroup. The calculations use successive one-base transitions, including within homozygous stretches.
:::

In a fuller genealogical model, recombination detaches a lineage and its subsequent re-coalescence depends on the previous genealogy and demographic history. That conditional process is more detailed than an independent epoch redraw.

### Forward likelihood and Viterbi decoding {#forward-and-viterbi}

Every intervening homozygous base must contribute to the observations. Using only the ten heterozygous sites would remove most of the information about heterozygosity density.

For emission probability $b_k(o)$, Forward uses

$$
\alpha_1(k)=\pi_kb_k(O_1),\qquad
\alpha_x(k)=b_k(O_x)\sum_l\alpha_{x-1}(l)A_{lk}(1).
$$

The observed-data likelihood is $\sum_k\alpha_L(k)$. The implementation normalizes the vector at each step and accumulates the logarithms of the normalization constants, preventing numerical underflow.

Viterbi instead uses a maximum over predecessors:

$$
\delta_1(k)=\log\pi_k+\log b_k(O_1),
$$

$$
\delta_x(k)=\log b_k(O_x)+\max_l\left[\delta_{x-1}(l)+\log A_{lk}(1)\right].
$$

Stored predecessor pointers allow backtracking to the most probable complete path. **Forward sums over hidden paths; Viterbi selects a single path.** Its joint path probability is not the observed-data likelihood.

The disagreement is understandable under this coarse model. Ten heterozygous bases among 51,359 observations give a density of approximately $1.95\times10^{-4}$, much lower than the E1 emission probability. The implementation was checked against exhaustive enumeration of all $3^4=81$ hidden paths for a four-base example. Both Forward likelihood and Viterbi decoding agreed with that check. The complete standard-library implementation is included in the [appendix](#psmc-light-code).

## Applying PSMC to human data {#question-3}

### Preprocessing and scaling {#empirical-methods}

I used the supplied GRCh38 variant files to construct diploid consensus sequences for chromosomes 1 and 21. The genotype calls determine the alleles, heterozygous SNPs are encoded with IUPAC ambiguity codes, missing calls are represented as unknown, and reference gaps remain unknown. The reference uses `chr1`/`chr21` while the VCF uses `1`/`21`, so sequence names were matched before consensus construction.

::: warning Callable-site limitation
The supplied individual files provide genotype calls without the per-sample depth and quality information required for a read-based callable-site filter. No independent callable-site mask was available in the inspected resources. Unlisted reference bases were therefore treated as homozygous wherever the reference was known. This is a teaching-data assumption, not evidence that every such base was callable; missed heterozygosity can affect demographic estimates.
:::

The consensus sequences were converted to PSMCFA using 100-bp bins. The completed runs used PSMC `0.6.5-r67` with:

```bash
psmc -N25 -t15 -r5 -p '4+25*2+4+6' \
    -o sample.chr1.psmc sample.chr1.psmcfa
```

All six baseline runs completed the configured 25 iterations. This stopping point alone does not establish statistical convergence or parameter identifiability.

For each result separately, I used

$$
s_{\mathrm{eff}}=100\times\mathrm{skip},\qquad
N_0=\frac{\theta_0}{4\mu s_{\mathrm{eff}}},
$$

$$
T_{k,\mathrm{years}}=2N_0(t_k+\mathrm{DT})g,\qquad
N_{e,k}=N_0\lambda_k.
$$

Here $\theta_0$ comes from `TR`, and $t_k$ and $\lambda_k$ from `RS`. All baseline `skip` values were 1; missing `DT` was treated as zero. The numerical exports were checked against the official [PSMC plotting script](https://github.com/lh3/psmc/blob/master/utils/psmc_plot.pl), accounting for its vertical-axis units.

The figures use post-step curves and logarithmic time axes. Zero time is not replaced with a fabricated positive value. For the baseline pattern, the official default omits the final parameter group, RS intervals 58–63; exported CSV files preserve all intervals. Curves end at the final retained point without extrapolation. Full-range figures retain the displayed extremes, while separate zoom views explicitly restrict the vertical range.

### X pseudodiploids and the supplied-data limitation {#q3-x-pseudodiploids}

The intended analysis combines one male YRI X sequence with one male CEU or CHB X sequence. In the non-PAR region, each male has one X copy, so differences between the two sequences define pseudodiploid heterozygosity. Sequence alleles must be compared at matching coordinates; concatenating two PSMCFA files does not construct a pseudodiploid.

In an idealized split model without subsequent gene flow, lineages sampled from different populations cannot coalesce more recently than their separation. A cross-population trajectory can therefore show very large recent apparent Ne. PSMC smoothing, finite sequence information, and migration complicate identifying a sharp split boundary. A curve upturn should not automatically be reported as an exact separation date.

Under the assignment's approximation,

$$
N_{e,X}=0.75N_{e,A},\qquad N_{e,A\text{-equivalent}}=\frac{N_{e,X}}{0.75}.
$$

This population-size conversion must be distinguished from the physical-time calibration. With a specified X mutation rate and generation time, time remains calculated as $2N_{0,X}t_Xg$; a size-ratio correction does not justify arbitrarily multiplying every physical time by 0.75.

The non-PAR X analysis used **GRCh37/b37 coordinates**, an assembly-matched [Ensembl X reference](https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/grch37/release-87/fasta/homo_sapiens/dna/Homo_sapiens.GRCh37.dna.chromosome.X.fa.gz), and the corresponding PAR boundaries. The chr1 and chr21 analyses used GRCh38.

::: table align="center" title="PAR intervals masked in the corrected GRCh37 X analysis"

| Region | One-based inclusive X coordinates |
| :---: | :---: |
| PAR1 | 60,001–2,699,520 |
| PAR2 | 154,931,044–155,260,560 |

:::

Both intervals were masked using the [GRCh37 PAR boundaries](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/grc/human).

::: note Different assemblies require matching inputs, not a universal reference
The assembly change affects this X analysis and its coordinate-based filtering. It does not alter the theoretical calculations in Questions 1 and 2, and does not by itself invalidate the completed chr1/chr21 runs, bootstraps, or pattern comparisons, which retain their original matching GRCh38 inputs and reference.

The resulting PSMC trajectories can still be discussed on their calibrated time axes, but X and autosomal results should not be presented as an otherwise matched experiment. Reference assembly, genomic coverage, filtering, and cross-population versus within-individual sampling all differ.
:::

#### X size correction and interpretation limits {#x-size-correction}

Applying the assignment's $N_{e,X}=0.75N_{e,A}$ approximation converts the vertical axis to an autosomal-equivalent apparent size by dividing by 0.75. This gives the corrected plateau values in the table above, while the raw X estimates remain available in the other figures and CSV files.

![X pseudodiploid curves expressed as autosomal-equivalent apparent size](https://pic.lucas04.top/1791534697184.png)

The time axis remains calibrated from each X curve's own theta, mutation rate, and generation time:

$$
N_{0,X}=\frac{\theta_0}{4\mu\,(100\times\mathrm{skip})},\qquad
T_{k,\mathrm{years}}=2N_{0,X}(t_k+\mathrm{DT})g.
$$

There is no additional multiplication or division of these physical times by 0.75. The vertical conversion is conditional on the assignment's size-ratio assumption; it does not turn a cross-population trajectory into the true size history of a single population.

Both spikes occupy RS4–5, immediately after the first four bound intervals. This makes the first-window mechanism discussed by [Hilgers et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2024.09.028) relevant to investigate. The earlier chr21 pattern analysis also showed a strongly parameter-sensitive recent YRI peak, but those autosomal results are not an X-specific sensitivity test. They motivate caution rather than prove that the X spikes are artifacts.

No alternative X patterns or X bootstrap runs have been performed. Splitting the first grouped window, resampling X blocks, and analysing additional independent cross-population pairs would help assess whether the observed transitions persist.

#### Resampling design {#bootstrap-methods}

For each YRI chromosome, I generated 100 block-bootstrap replicates. The existing PSMCFA sequence was divided into blocks of 5,000 characters, corresponding to 500 kb at 100 bp per character:

```bash

```

#### Comparing uncertainty at the same physical time {#bootstrap-statistics}

Different replicate scale estimates produce different physical time boundaries, even when RS indices match. I therefore evaluated post-step Ne values on a shared 2,000-point logarithmic grid within the common displayed support of all 200 bootstrap curves and both baselines: approximately **4.496 ka–4.059 Ma**. Baselines were excluded from the bootstrap summary statistics, leaving 100 values per chromosome per point.

For each physical time, the sample variance uses denominator 99:

$$
s^2(t)=\frac{1}{99}\sum_{b=1}^{100}\left[N_e^{(b)}(t)-\overline N_e(t)\right]^2,
\qquad
\operatorname{CV}(t)=\frac{s(t)}{\overline N_e(t)}.
$$

Median, standard deviation, and empirical 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles were also computed. Time evaluation uses the left-boundary Ne for each step, with no linear interpolation or extrapolation.

![YRI bootstrap comparison, zoomed](https://pic.lucas04.top/1791444002753.png)

![Absolute and relative bootstrap uncertainty](https://pic.lucas04.top/1791444031635.png)

Across the common logarithmic grid, chr21 exceeds chr1 at **99.2% of points for variance**, **97.6% for CV**, and **98.85% for percentile-interval width**. These percentages describe the selected grid, not independent hypothesis tests or proportions of linear calendar time. There are reversals at some older times, so chr21 is not more uncertain under every metric at every point.

The overall difference is consistent with chr21 providing fewer blocks and less genealogical information. Absolute variance, measured in Ne², must be distinguished from CV, which describes relative spread. A higher mean Ne can contribute to larger absolute variability without the same change in relative variability.

#### Separating parameter freedom from time discretization {#pattern-controlled-comparisons}

**p25x2 versus p50x1** keeps the number of atomic intervals at 64 while increasing free Ne parameters from 28 to 53. The baseline YRI high platform is split into two platforms, including a much more extreme peak. This illustrates sensitivity to local parameter freedom. Physical boundaries are still slightly different because the fitted scaling is not identical.

**p50x1 versus p50x2** keeps 53 free Ne parameters but changes the atomic grid from 64 to 114 intervals. Despite identical parameter counts, the recent YRI results differ substantially. The group coverage, time discretization, and fitted scaling all matter. More atomic intervals therefore do not invariably produce a more extreme peak: p50x2 is less extreme here than p50x1.

The quantitative comparisons were limited to the four-pattern common displayed support within each sample: approximately 7.689 ka–3.205 Ma for YRI, 5.237 ka–2.504 Ma for CEU, and 4.718 ka–2.299 Ma for CHB. Values outside these ranges were not extrapolated.

Coarse settings can conceal local changes and finer settings can permit additional fluctuations, but these observations do not establish a universally optimal pattern. Training likelihood alone is not proof of demographic accuracy, and the p25x2 bootstrap intervals cannot be transferred to other patterns.

### Discussion in light of Hilgers et al. (2025) {#paper-discussion}

The assigned paper, [_Avoidable false PSMC population size peaks occur across numerous studies_](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2024.09.028), identifies a more specific problem than simply using too many parameters.

Hilgers and colleagues show that binding the first four atomic intervals into one Ne parameter can prevent the model from representing a recent decline within that window. The model can compensate by inferring an exaggerated population-size peak in the adjacent older window. They found such peaks in real data and simulations, and removed them in several datasets by replacing the initial `4` with `2+2`, or, when needed, `1+1+1+1`.

The paper also reports that apparently consistent bootstrap support did not rule out these artifacts. Resampling robustness and robustness to parameterization address different questions. Population structure provides another possible source of elevated inferred Ne, because reduced coalescence between structured populations can be interpreted as larger size under a panmictic model.

Our YRI baseline peak begins at the boundary after the first four atomic intervals, approximately 17.686 ka, which makes the paper's mechanism relevant to investigate. It is also sensitive to the tested pattern and unstable under block resampling. These observations justify caution about interpreting its height and duration as a population expansion, but they do not establish its cause.

::: warning The four requested patterns do not test the proposed correction
All four patterns retain the initial `4`. Changing the middle interval groups does **not** directly test the paper's intervention of splitting the first window. The current results establish parameter sensitivity, not confirmation of that specific artifact or proof that it has been removed.
:::

A direct follow-up would use `2+2+25*2+4+6` and, if necessary, `1+1+1+1+25*2+4+6`, holding the remaining settings and input constant. These additional runs have **not** been performed. The paper also checks that parameter windows contain sufficient inferred recombination information; the finite-value and iteration checks used here do not replace that assessment.

The human examples in the paper use $\mu=1.25\times10^{-8}$, whereas this assignment uses $2.5\times10^{-8}$. Their physical time and Ne axes therefore cannot be compared directly without reconciling scaling. The mechanism and sensitivity argument remain relevant even when the numerical scales differ.

## Interpretation and remaining work {#limitations}

The analytical exercise shows how a short bottleneck can have a high coalescence rate while accounting for a minority of all coalescence events. The PSMC-light calculation shows why sparse mutations and state persistence can favour a decoded path that disagrees with the supplied genealogy.

In the empirical analyses, broad demographic features recur across chromosomes and parameter settings more consistently than the recent YRI chr21 peak. Bootstrap variability is generally greater on chr21, but absolute and relative uncertainty should be reported separately. Parameter sensitivity and the paper's first-window artifact prevent a strong biological claim about that peak.

The non-PAR X pseudodiploid construction and PSMC comparison are complete. The fitted transitions occur around 22–33 ka for YRI–CEU and 22–32 ka for YRI–CHB, but population separation times remain uncertain. X-specific parameter sensitivity analyses and block bootstraps are needed to assess the stability of these features.

## Appendix: Reproducing the PSMC-light calculation {#psmc-light-code}

Save the following code as `psmc_light.py`. It uses only the Python standard library, checks a four-base example by exhaustive enumeration, and writes parameter results, the ten-site comparison, and the full per-base decoded path.

```bash
python3 psmc_light.py --outdir results_between_hets
python3 psmc_light.py --start 1 --outdir results_from_bp1
```

The coefficient multiplying $T d$ defaults to $2r=2\times10^{-8}$. It can be changed explicitly if a different convention is specified, but the numerical answers above refer to this default.

::: collapse

- Complete Python implementation

  ```python
  """Three-epoch assignment: forward and Viterbi, Python standard library only.

  Physical times are generations; recombination exponent is 2*r*T*d.
  The main observation interval is first het through last het, inclusive.
  """
  import argparse
  import csv
  import itertools
  import json
  import math
  from pathlib import Path

  EPOCHS = [3, 2, 1]
  POSITIONS = [5426, 8777, 12746, 15828, 16431, 18116, 26240, 54783, 55675, 56784]
  TRUTH = [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 1]


  def parameters(mu=2.5e-8, r=1e-8):
    rates = [1 / 40000, 1 / 4000, 1 / 20000]
    pi = [
      -math.expm1(-0.05), math.exp(-0.05) * -math.expm1(-0.125),
      math.exp(-0.175)
    ]
    times = [40000 - 2000 / math.expm1(0.05),
      2000 + 4000 - 500 / math.expm1(0.125), 22500.0]
    het = [-math.expm1(-2 * mu * t) for t in times]
    return rates, pi, times, het


  def transition(pi, times, coefficient, distance=1):
    # coefficient=2*r when times are measured in generations.
    q = [math.exp(-coefficient * t * distance) for t in times]
    return [[q[i] * (i == j) + (1 - q[i]) * pi[j]
      for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]


  def emission(observation, het):
    return het if observation else [1 - h for h in het]


  def forward(observations, pi, het, matrix):
    ("Scaled forward recursion: return log likelihood "
      "and final filtering distribution.")
    alpha = None
    log_likelihood = 0.0
    for observation in observations:
      b = emission(observation, het)
      if alpha is None:
        alpha = [pi[j] * b[j] for j in range(3)]
      else:
        alpha = [b[j] * sum(alpha[i] * matrix[i][j] for i in range(3))
          for j in range(3)]
      scale = sum(alpha)
      if scale <= 0:
        raise ValueError('Zero likelihood')
      log_likelihood += math.log(scale)
      alpha = [value / scale for value in alpha]
    return log_likelihood, alpha


  def viterbi(observations, pi, het, matrix):
    ("Log-space Viterbi with predecessor pointers; "
      "return path and joint log probability.")
    log_matrix = [[math.log(value) for value in row] for row in matrix]
    b = emission(observations[0], het)
    delta = [math.log(pi[j]) + math.log(b[j]) for j in range(3)]
    predecessors = []
    for observation in observations[1:]:
      b = emission(observation, het)
      next_delta, previous = [], []
      for j in range(3):
        candidates = [delta[i] + log_matrix[i][j] for i in range(3)]
        best = max(range(3), key=lambda i: candidates[i])
        previous.append(best)
        next_delta.append(candidates[best] + math.log(b[j]))
      delta = next_delta
      predecessors.append(previous)
    state = max(range(3), key=lambda j: delta[j])
    log_joint = delta[state]
    path = [state]
    for previous in reversed(predecessors):
      state = previous[state]
      path.append(state)
    return path[::-1], log_joint


  def check_algorithms(pi, het, matrix):
    ("Compare both recursions with all 3**4 paths "
      "on a small observation sequence.")
    observations = [0, 1, 0, 1]
    joint_probabilities = []
    paths = list(itertools.product(range(3), repeat=len(observations)))
    for path in paths:
      probability = pi[path[0]] * emission(observations[0], het)[path[0]]
      for x in range(1, len(path)):
        probability *= matrix[path[x - 1]][path[x]]
        probability *= emission(observations[x], het)[path[x]]
      joint_probabilities.append(probability)
    log_likelihood, _ = forward(observations, pi, het, matrix)
    best_path, log_joint = viterbi(observations, pi, het, matrix)
    assert math.isclose(
      math.exp(log_likelihood), sum(joint_probabilities), rel_tol=1e-12
    )
    best_index = max(range(len(paths)), key=lambda i: joint_probabilities[i])
    assert tuple(best_path) == paths[best_index]
    assert math.isclose(
      math.exp(log_joint), joint_probabilities[best_index], rel_tol=1e-12
    )


  def analyze(start, pi, het, matrix):
    het_positions = set(POSITIONS)
    observations = [
      int(x in het_positions) for x in range(start, POSITIONS[-1] + 1)
    ]
    log_likelihood, filtering = forward(observations, pi, het, matrix)
    path, log_joint = viterbi(observations, pi, het, matrix)
    decoded = [EPOCHS[path[position - start]] for position in POSITIONS]
    runs = []
    run_start = start
    for index in range(1, len(path)):
      if path[index] != path[index - 1]:
        runs.append({'start_bp': run_start, 'end_bp': start + index - 1,
          'epoch': EPOCHS[path[index - 1]]})
        run_start = start + index
    runs.append({
      'start_bp': run_start, 'end_bp': POSITIONS[-1],
      'epoch': EPOCHS[path[-1]]
    })
    return {'start_bp': start, 'end_bp': POSITIONS[-1],
        'n_sites': len(observations),
        'n_heterozygous': sum(observations), 'log_likelihood': log_likelihood,
        'likelihood': math.exp(log_likelihood),
        'viterbi_joint_log_probability': log_joint,
        'viterbi_epochs_at_het_sites': decoded, 'true_epochs_at_het_sites': TRUTH,
        'correct_at_het_sites': sum(a == b for a, b in zip(decoded, TRUTH)),
        'final_filtering_distribution': filtering, 'viterbi_runs': runs}, path


  def main():
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    parser.add_argument('--start', type=int, default=5426, choices=[1, 5426])
    parser.add_argument('--outdir', type=Path, default=Path('results'))
    parser.add_argument('--recombination-coefficient', type=float, default=2e-8,
      help=('Coefficient multiplying T*d in the exponent; '
        'physical default is 2*r'))
    args = parser.parse_args()
    if args.recombination_coefficient <= 0:
      parser.error('Recombination coefficient must be positive')
    rates, pi, times, het = parameters()
    matrix = transition(pi, times, args.recombination_coefficient)
    assert math.isclose(sum(pi), 1.0, abs_tol=1e-12)
    for row in matrix:
      assert math.isclose(sum(row), 1.0, abs_tol=1e-12)
    check_algorithms(pi, het, matrix)
    result, path = analyze(args.start, pi, het, matrix)
    result.update({
      'epoch_order': EPOCHS, 'coalescence_rates': rates,
      'initial_distribution': pi,
      'conditional_mean_times_generations': times,
      'heterozygosity_probabilities': het,
      'transition_matrix_per_bp': matrix,
      'recombination_coefficient_per_bp_per_generation':
        args.recombination_coefficient,
      'brute_force_algorithm_check': 'passed'
    })
    args.outdir.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    (args.outdir / 'results.json').write_text(json.dumps(result, indent=2) + '\n')
    with (args.outdir / 'heterozygous_sites.csv').open('w', newline='') as handle:
      writer = csv.writer(handle)
      writer.writerow(['position_bp', 'true_epoch', 'viterbi_epoch'])
      writer.writerows(
        zip(POSITIONS, TRUTH, result['viterbi_epochs_at_het_sites'])
      )
    with (args.outdir / 'viterbi_path.csv').open('w', newline='') as handle:
      writer = csv.writer(handle)
      writer.writerow(['position_bp', 'observation', 'viterbi_epoch'])
      het_positions = set(POSITIONS)
      writer.writerows(
        (args.start + i, int(args.start + i in het_positions), EPOCHS[s])
        for i, s in enumerate(path)
      )
    print(json.dumps(result, indent=2))


  if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
  ```
