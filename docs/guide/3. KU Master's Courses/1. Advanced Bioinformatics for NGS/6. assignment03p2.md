## Methods and scope {#methods}

I used **hmmix**, a hidden Markov model (HMM) approach, to identify candidate archaic segments in three phased modern human genomes from private SNP density. I then compared the inferred segments with Neanderthal and Denisovan references. Detection of candidate archaic ancestry and assignment to a particular archaic source were treated as separate inference steps.

The analysis examined the effects of incomplete callability and regional mutation-rate variation on model training, compared three decoding methods and posterior path sampling, and assessed haplotype overlap and similarity to archaic references.

::: note Scope of the results

The individuals are NA19078, NA20810, and NA21130, with East Asian, European, and South Asian ancestry labels in the assignment. Results describe these three individuals, not population-wide estimates. Observations contain no chrX sites, so ancestry results below concern the represented autosomal sequences.

All reported archaic totals sum the lengths on both phased haplotypes. A position called archaic on both copies contributes twice to that total. Shared-coordinate lengths, reported separately, count the position once.

:::

## Private SNP observations and corrections {#observations-and-corrections}

### Observation format and private SNP counts

Each observation row records a private SNP retained after filtering against an outgroup:

```text
chrom   pos      ancestral_base   genotype
chr1    915818   G                AG
chr1    981119   G                GA
```

The two characters in `genotype` correspond to the two phased haplotypes. At the first site, the ancestral base is G, so haplotype 1 carries the derived allele A and haplotype 2 carries G. At the second site, the derived allele occurs on haplotype 2. Thus, `AG` and `GA` are different observations for haplotype-specific inference.

“Private” means relative to the sampled outgroup. It does not mean unique to this individual, and it does not establish that a SNP is archaic.

::: table align="center" title="Individuals and retained private SNP sites"

| Individual | Ancestry label | Private SNP sites |
|   :---:    |     :---:      |       :---:       |
|  NA19078   |   East Asian   |      125,698      |
|  NA20810   |    European    |      115,115      |
|  NA21130   |  South Asian   |      130,662      |

:::

NA21130 has the most retained sites, but this does not directly rank archaic ancestry. Population history, diversity, relationships to the outgroup, introgression, and technical filtering can all affect the count. The HMM uses the spatial distribution of these sites rather than their genome-wide total alone.

### Callability correction

The hg38 callability mask contains **4,113,020 intervals** across chromosomes 1–22 and X. These fragmented intervals identify sequence in which variants can be detected reliably. Repetitive sequence, ambiguous mapping, low coverage, poor base quality, and structural variation can reduce callability.

The union of callable intervals covers **2,280,080,701 bp**, or approximately 2.280 Gb. Using approximately 3.03 Gb for chromosomes 1–22 and X gives:

$$
f_{\mathrm{excluded}}
=1-\frac{2.280080701}{3.03}
\approx 0.2475.
$$

Approximately **75.25% is callable and 24.75% is excluded** by this mask. This denominator describes the mask’s chromosome coverage, including X; it is distinct from the autosomal ancestry analysis.

Without this correction, a window with few observed SNPs could be interpreted as having low mutation density even when much of its sequence was not observable. That can distort emissions, state transitions, and inferred segment boundaries.

### Regional mutation-rate variation

The mutation-rate file contains 3,043 windows of 1 Mb across chromosomes 1–22 and X. Its fourth column is a **relative mutation-rate factor**, not an absolute per-base, per-generation mutation rate.

Across the 249 chr1 windows:

::: table align="center" title="Relative mutation-rate factors in chr1 windows"

|       Statistic        | Relative mutation-rate factor |
|         :---:          |             :---:             |
|         Median         |              0.93             |
|         P5–P95         |            0–1.316            |
| Minimum positive value |              0.68             |
|        Maximum         |             39.68             |
|  Zero-valued windows   |               19              |

:::

The maximum is approximately **58.35 times the minimum positive value**. The P5–P95 range includes zeros, so it should not be read as a biological mutation-rate range for reliably observed sequence. The causes of the zero values and the extreme value cannot be established from this file alone.

![Relative mutation-rate factors along chr1, shown on linear and logarithmic scales.](https://pic.lucas04.top/1791558230763.png)

*Relative mutation-rate factors in 1 Mb chr1 windows. The logarithmic panel omits zero values rather than adding a pseudocount.*

A schematic expression for the expected private SNP count in window $w$ is

$$
E[K_w\mid Z_w=s]\approx\lambda_s c_w m_w,
$$

where $\lambda_s$ is the state-specific baseline emission rate, $c_w$ is callable sequence measured in the model’s window units, and $m_w$ is the local relative mutation-rate factor.

High mutation rates can produce more private SNPs even within modern human ancestry. Without correction, that signal may be mistaken for archaic ancestry; conversely, archaic sequence in a low-rate region may be missed.

## Model training {#model-training}

The two states are **Human** and **Archaic**. I trained one model per individual using the callability and mutation-rate files, with `-haploid` to process phased haplotypes separately. A representative command is:

```fish
hmmix train \
    -obs=NA19078_obs.txt \
    -weights=hg38_strick_callability_mask.bed \
    -mutrates=hg38_mutationrate.bed \
    -haploid \
    -out=trained.NA19078.json
```

::: table align="center" title="Fitted HMM parameters by individual"

|           Parameter            |  NA19078  |  NA20810  |  NA21130  |
|             :---:              |   :---:   |   :---:   |   :---:   |
|  Initial Archaic probability   |  0.02489  |  0.01993  |  0.02244  |
|        Human → Archaic         | 0.0005355 | 0.0004748 | 0.0004880 |
|        Archaic → Human         |  0.02097  |  0.02335  |  0.02126  |
|      Human emission rate       |  0.02249  |  0.02111  |  0.02392  |
|     Archaic emission rate      |  0.32559  |  0.32328  |  0.32905  |
| Archaic / Human emission ratio |   14.48   |   15.31   |   13.76   |

:::

The fitted parameters are broadly similar. NA21130 has the highest emissions in both states, while NA20810 has the lowest. NA19078 has the highest Human-to-Archaic and lowest Archaic-to-Human transition probabilities, implying slightly longer expected Archaic-state runs.

Archaic emissions are approximately **14–15 times** Human emissions. Compared with the practical’s literature benchmarks of $\lambda_H\approx0.03$ and $\lambda_A\approx0.3$, the Human estimates are slightly lower and the Archaic estimates slightly higher. The basic distinction remains the same: archaic regions are enriched for private SNPs.

::: warning Initial probabilities are not decoded ancestry fractions

The initial Archaic probability describes the model’s starting-state distribution. It should not be interpreted directly as the proportion of an individual’s genome assigned archaic ancestry. That quantity must be calculated from decoded segments or posterior uncertainty.

:::

### Effects of removing the corrections

I repeated training for NA19078, removing one correction at a time.

::: table align="center" title="Effects of removing corrections for NA19078"

|          Parameter          | Full model | Without callability mask | Without mutation-rate correction |
|            :---:            |   :---:    |          :---:           |              :---:               |
|        Human emission       |  0.02249   |  $3.47\times10^{-178}$   |             0.02276              |
|       Archaic emission      |  0.32559   |         0.08894          |             0.33154              |
|       Human → Archaic       | 0.0005355  |         0.03812          |            0.0005474             |
|       Archaic → Human       |  0.02097   |         0.10372          |             0.02106              |
| Initial Archaic probability |  0.02489   |         0.26877          |             0.02533              |

:::

Removing the mask radically changes the model. The Human emission collapses to essentially zero, the Archaic emission falls by approximately 72.7%, and the two switching probabilities increase approximately 71-fold and 5-fold. This suggests that the states may be separating SNP-free and SNP-containing regions rather than preserving their intended ancestry interpretation. The state labels in the output therefore do not establish that the fitted states retain their intended biological interpretation.

Removing mutation-rate correction has a smaller effect on global parameters: Human and Archaic emissions increase by approximately 1.18% and 1.83%. However, stable global estimates do not demonstrate that local ancestry calls are unchanged. Regional rate variation can still influence particular boundaries or classifications.

## Comparison of decoding methods {#decoding}

I applied posterior decoding, Viterbi, and hybrid decoding with $\alpha=0.8$ to each full model. Posterior decoding chooses the most probable state at each window; Viterbi chooses the most probable complete path. Hybrid decoding balances complete-path and marginal-state objectives.

::: table align="center" title="Total archaic sequence length by decoding method"

| Individual | Posterior total (Mb) | Hybrid total (Mb) | Viterbi total (Mb) |
|   :---:    |        :---:         |       :---:       |       :---:        |
|  NA19078   |       116.973        |      111.184      |      101.635       |
|  NA20810   |        91.806        |       86.909      |       78.732       |
|  NA21130   |       104.696        |       99.465      |       91.740       |

:::

All methods rank NA19078 highest and NA20810 lowest. Within each individual, total length follows **Posterior > Hybrid > Viterbi**. The figure compares these hard-decoding totals with the posterior-sampling mean and approximate 95% posterior interval (P2.5–P97.5), described below. All lengths are in Mb and sum both phased haplotypes for each individual.

### Posterior sampling and total-length uncertainty

For each individual, I sampled 100 hidden paths from the posterior using `hmmix inhomogeneous -samples=100`. Each sample has a haplotype-1 and haplotype-2 segment file; their archaic lengths were paired by sample number and summed.

::: table align="center" title="Posterior-sampled total archaic sequence lengths"

| Individual | Sample mean (Mb) | Median (Mb) |   Min–max (Mb)  | P2.5–P97.5 (Mb) |
|   :---:    |      :---:       |    :---:    |      :---:      |      :---:      |
|  NA19078   |     142.796      |   142.755   | 140.019–146.136 | 140.618–145.817 |
|  NA20810   |     114.400      |   114.294   | 112.228–117.109 | 112.454–116.482 |
|  NA21130   |     129.119      |   129.245   | 125.318–131.605 | 126.504–131.047 |

:::

The sampled totals exceed all three hard-decoding totals. This difference follows from the distinction between posterior sampling and hard classification. A window with an Archaic probability of 0.1 is assigned Human by posterior decoding, but is Archaic in approximately one tenth of posterior draws. Across many uncertain windows, those contributions accumulate.

For equal-sized windows, the posterior expected total length is conceptually

$$
E[T_A\mid x,\hat\theta]
=\sum_{h,w}\Delta_w P(Z_{h,w}=A\mid x,\hat\theta),
$$

whereas posterior hard calls sum only windows whose Archaic probability exceeds 0.5. There is no requirement that the latter total lie inside an interval for sampled totals.

Some sampled Archaic segments have low marginal support or no observed SNPs. They should not be removed by an additional probability filter: doing so would change the sampled distribution. In weakly informed regions, the inferred paths reflect both data and model assumptions.

The P2.5–P97.5 ranges are approximate **95% posterior intervals conditional on the fitted parameters**. They do not include uncertainty in parameter estimation, model misspecification, or reference construction.

Under the simplified relationship

$$
E[L]=\frac{1}{gr},
$$

$g$ is generations since admixture and $r$ is recombination probability per base per generation when $L$ is measured in bases. At the same recombination rate, shorter surviving tracts suggest older admixture because recombination has had more generations to fragment them.

NA20810 has the shortest mean under every decoder. This is consistent with older admixture under the simplified model, but it is not a dating result. Decoder choice itself changes mean lengths substantially: Viterbi produces fewer and longer segments in all three individuals. Local recombination, demographic history, selection, and detection of short segments further complicate inference.

### Coordinate-wise containment of decoded segments

I tested containment separately for each haplotype by calculating the interval differences Hybrid minus Posterior and Viterbi minus Hybrid. Neither difference was zero in any of the six haplotypes.

These lengths sum exceptions across both haplotypes. Although small relative to overall totals, they disprove strict containment of the form Posterior $\supseteq$ Hybrid $\supseteq$ Viterbi for these results.

Seven observation sites occur within the posterior-called interval, but only six carry a derived allele on haplotype 1. The SNP concentration provides local support for an Archaic segment. Viterbi nevertheless favours a complete path remaining Human, balancing emission evidence against transition costs. Marginal support near 0.78 does not guarantee inclusion in the most probable joint path. Hybrid retains the local signal with a slightly shorter boundary. These tracks show local calls only; their apparent ordering does not imply strict genome-wide containment, as demonstrated by the interval differences above.

Here, Jaccard is intersection length divided by union length. The shared fraction instead divides that intersection by one haplotype’s archaic length.

Under posterior decoding, approximately 85–89% of each haplotype’s archaic length is not called archaic at the same position on the other copy. All three decoders show the same qualitative pattern.

Partial overlap is expected because the two haplotypes come from different parents and carry combinations of introgressed sequence shaped by inheritance and recombination. Some positions can be archaic on both homologous chromosomes. An ancestry call alone does not establish that those copies carry an identical archaic haplotype.

## Annotation against archaic references {#archaic-annotation}

The detection model did not require an archaic genome. Annotation subsequently compared the retained derived alleles with Altai, Vindija, and Chagyrskaya Neanderthals and a Denisovan reference. The existing full-model parameters were retained.

Five additional columns summarize matching SNPs:

::: table align="center" title="Archaic-reference annotation columns"

|         Column        |                                             Meaning                                             |
|         :---:         |                                              :---:                                              |
|   `admixpopvariants`  | SNPs whose derived allele is present in at least one archaic reference, counting each site once |
|   `AltaiNeandertal`   |                                   Matching SNP count for Altai                                  |
|     `Vindija33.19`    |                                  Matching SNP count for Vindija                                 |
|       `Denisova`      |                                 Matching SNP count for Denisovan                                |
| `Chagyrskaya-Phalanx` |                                Matching SNP count for Chagyrskaya                               |

:::

These are counts, not fractions. A SNP can match several references, so the reference-specific columns overlap and cannot simply be summed. Matching requires the reference to carry the derived allele; it does not require homozygosity for that allele. This counting behaviour is implemented in hmmix’s [annotation helper](https://github.com/LauritsSkov/Introgression-detection/blob/4419a2359eca8c855deb8bc6bab038e7e413983e/src/helper_functions.py) and [output writer](https://github.com/LauritsSkov/Introgression-detection/blob/4419a2359eca8c855deb8bc6bab038e7e413983e/src/hmm_functions.py).

For NA19078 haplotype 1 at chr1:2,436,000–2,474,000, 16 of 19 private SNPs match at least one reference. Altai, Vindija, and Chagyrskaya match 12, 16, and 15 sites, while Denisova matches one. This is strong relative support for a Neanderthal-like label.

By contrast, the 15 kb decoder-disagreement segment discussed above has zero matches to all four references. It remains unclear: lack of matching evidence does not by itself establish either a particular source or a false positive.

### Segment classification rule

The assignment leaves the classification threshold open. I used a conservative exploratory rule, defining

$$
N=\max(n_{\mathrm{Altai}},n_{\mathrm{Vindija}},n_{\mathrm{Chagyrskaya}}),
\qquad D=n_{\mathrm{Denisova}}.
$$

A segment is **Neanderthal-like** if $N>D$, $N\ge3$, and $N$ matches at least 50% of its private SNPs. It is **Denisovan-like** under the analogous conditions for $D$. Ties, insufficient matches, and zero matches are **unclear**.

Taking the maximum avoids adding overlapping counts, but is not the union of Neanderthal-matching sites. This heuristic is not a calibrated ancestry classifier, and using several Neanderthal references versus one Denisovan reference introduces asymmetry in the comparison.

## Discussion and limitations {#interpretation}

The analysis distinguishes three kinds of inference: parameters that describe SNP density and ancestry continuity, paths that assign ancestry along chromosomes, and reference comparisons that suggest possible sources. Each has its own uncertainty.

The strongest methodological result is the importance of callability. Removing the mask changes the fitted states so dramatically that their ancestry labels become difficult to defend. The decoder comparison shows a different limitation: a longer total does not guarantee coordinate-wise containment, and a locally probable segment need not occur in the most probable complete path.

Posterior sampling preserves uncertainty discarded by hard calls, while reference annotation adds evidence about source similarity. Neither removes dependence on the fitted model or the chosen classification rule. The inferred tracts should therefore be interpreted as model-dependent candidates, with reference similarity providing additional evidence rather than definitive donor assignments.
