## Ancestral state of the OBP gene family size {#question1}

The single-lambda analysis uses the OBP copy-number table and the dated Hexapoda tree. With `INPUT_DIR` and `OUTPUT_DIR` set to the input and output directories, respectively, a single-lambda command can be written as:

```bash
mkdir -p "$OUTPUT_DIR"
cafe -i "$INPUT_DIR/obp_all.hexapoda.tsv" \
  -t "$INPUT_DIR/hexapoda.nwk" \
  -y "$INPUT_DIR/hexapoda_2lambdamodel.nwk" \
  -o "$OUTPUT_DIR/single_lambda" 2>&1 \
  | tee "$OUTPUT_DIR/single_lambda.log"
```

CAFE estimated **30 OBP gene copies** at the root of the Hexapoda phylogeny (node `<19>`), as reported in `Base_count.tab`. This is the inferred family size in the common ancestor of the 18 sampled species. Present-day family sizes range from **5 to 83 copies**.

## Evolution of the OBP gene family {#question2}

The OBP gene family shows a **birth-and-death pattern of evolution**, with repeated gene gains and losses. The reconstruction indicates successive expansions along the lineage leading to *Drosophila*, whose inferred common ancestor had 49 copies. Most surveyed *Drosophila* species have broadly similar family sizes of 41–55 copies, although *D. willistoni* has a further expansion to 62 copies.

The main lineage-specific changes are shown below. Each change is calculated relative to the branch's **immediate ancestor**, rather than to the root of the phylogeny.

::: table align="center" title="Selected OBP expansions and contractions inferred by the single-lambda model"

| Lineage | Ancestral copies | Descendant copies | Change | Reported branch probability |
| :---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| *Anopheles gambiae* | 44 | 83 | +39 | 0.0000808 |
| *Drosophila willistoni* | 49 | 62 | +13 | 0.00507 |
| *Tribolium castaneum* | 40 | 49 | +9 | 0.2546 |
| *Bombyx mori* | 43 | 46 | +3 | 0.6194 |
| *Apis mellifera* | 36 | 21 | −15 | 0.0695 |
| Common ancestor of *P. humanus* and *A. pisum* | 30 | 21 | −9 | 0.0296 |
| *Pediculus humanus* | 21 | 5 | −16 | 0.00133 |
| *Acyrthosiphon pisum* | 21 | 18 | −3 | 0.7676 |

:::

The largest terminal expansion occurs in *A. gambiae*, with 39 additional copies relative to its inferred ancestor. *D. willistoni* also shows a pronounced expansion, whereas *T. castaneum* and *B. mori* show smaller increases. In contrast, *A. mellifera* has lost 15 copies relative to its ancestor. The lineage shared by *P. humanus* and *A. pisum* first contracts to 21 copies, followed by a further substantial reduction to five copies in *P. humanus*.

Thus, ==the OBP family has undergone lineage-specific expansions and contractions across Hexapoda==. The reported branch probabilities are below the nominal 0.05 threshold for the expansions in *A. gambiae* and *D. willistoni*, the contraction in *P. humanus*, and the contraction in the shared ancestor of *P. humanus* and *A. pisum*. These results have not been corrected for multiple testing. The contraction in *A. mellifera* is substantial in copy number but does not meet this nominal threshold.

Different copy-number changes do not necessarily imply different evolutionary rates. The rate comparison is addressed in [Question 3](#question3).

## Comparison of single- and two-lambda models {#question3}

To test whether the OBP birth-and-death rate differs between *Drosophila* and the other sampled hexapods, I used the same copy-number table and dated phylogeny together with the supplied lambda-assignment tree:

```bash
mkdir -p "$OUTPUT_DIR"
cafe -i "$INPUT_DIR/obp_all.hexapoda.tsv" \
  -t "$INPUT_DIR/hexapoda.nwk" \
  -y "$INPUT_DIR/hexapoda_2lambdamodel.nwk" \
  -o "$OUTPUT_DIR/two_lambda" 2>&1 \
  | tee "$OUTPUT_DIR/two_lambda.log"
```

In the lambda-assignment tree, branches within and leading to the *Drosophila* clade are labelled `1`, whereas the other hexapod branches are labelled `2`. CAFE therefore estimated the following rates:

::: table align="center" title="Comparison of the single- and two-lambda CAFE models"

| Model | Rate assignment | Estimated $\lambda$ | Model Base Final Likelihood ($-\ln L$) |
| :---: | :--- | :---: | ---: |
| Single-lambda | All branches | 0.00333333 | 81.0423 |
| Two-lambda | *Drosophila* | 0.00500013 | 80.6524 |
| Two-lambda | Other Hexapoda | 0.00321654 | 80.6524 |

:::

The estimated rate in *Drosophila* is approximately **1.55 times** that in the other sampled hexapods, suggesting faster OBP turnover under the two-rate model. The two-lambda model has a higher likelihood: its $-\ln L$ is 80.6524, compared with 81.0423 for the single-lambda model, an improvement of 0.3899.

Thus, the two-rate estimates suggest that OBP birth-and-death rates may vary across the phylogeny. However, the improvement is small, so this comparison does not establish a significant rate difference. As allowed by the assignment, I did not perform a simulation-based significance test. The single-lambda estimate also reached the reported upper limit, which adds uncertainty to the comparison.

## Detecting positive selection in OBP genes {#question4}

Due to an IT issue with the Emily server, I ran the analysis on my local machine. I installed HyPhy through `micromamba` and used the following version:

```text
HYPHY 2.5.103(MP) for Darwin on arm64 ARM Neon SIMD zlib (v1.3.2)
```

I ran aBSREL on all branches so that the terminal branches of the three duplicated genes were included in the analysis:

```bash
mkdir -p "$OUTPUT_DIR/absrel"

hyphy absrel \
  --alignment "$INPUT_DIR/Obp_aln_absrel.fas" \
  --tree "$INPUT_DIR/Obp_tree.nwk" \
  --branches All \
  --output "$OUTPUT_DIR/absrel/Obp_absrel.json" \
  2>&1 | tee "$OUTPUT_DIR/absrel/Obp_absrel.log"
```

The alignment contains five sequences and 102 codons. For each of the three duplicated genes, aBSREL selected a single $\omega=dN/dS$ rate class in the final adaptive model:

::: table align="center" title="aBSREL estimates for the duplicated OBP terminal branches"

| Gene branch | Estimated $\omega=dN/dS$ | Proportion of sites | LRT | Corrected $p$-value |
| :---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `Obp56f` | 0.4222 | 100% | 0 | 1.0 |
| `Obp56g` | 0.1575 | 100% | 0 | 1.0 |
| `Obp56h` | 0.2563 | 100% | 0 | 1.0 |

:::

The estimated dN/dS ratios are approximately **0.42 for OBP56f, 0.16 for OBP56g, and 0.26 for OBP56h**. All three are below one, consistent with purifying selection. These values come from the final aBSREL model in `Obp_absrel.json`.

## Differences in functional constraint {#question5}

`Obp56f` has the highest estimated dN/dS ratio ($\omega=0.4222$), followed by `Obp56h` ($\omega=0.2563$) and `Obp56g` ($\omega=0.1575$). This suggests **weaker purifying constraint in OBP56f** and stronger constraint in OBP56g, although the differences were not formally tested.

A plausible explanation is functional redundancy after gene duplication. If another copy maintains the original function, some amino-acid changes in a duplicate may become less harmful, allowing it to diverge. This could eventually lead to different functions among copies, but the dN/dS estimates alone cannot show whether that has happened.

## Genes and sites under positive selection {#question6}

I used MEME to test individual codon sites for episodic diversifying selection across all branches, with a threshold of $p\leq0.1$ and bootstrap resampling disabled:

```bash
mkdir -p "$OUTPUT_DIR/meme"

hyphy meme \
  --alignment "$INPUT_DIR/Obp_aln_meme.fas" \
  --tree "$INPUT_DIR/Obp_tree.nwk" \
  --branches All \
  --pvalue 0.1 \
  --resample 0 \
  --output "$OUTPUT_DIR/meme/Obp_meme.json" \
  2>&1 | tee "$OUTPUT_DIR/meme/Obp_meme.log"
```

The alignment contains five sequences and 174 codons. MEME identified three candidate sites at $p\leq0.1$. Site numbers refer to codon positions in the input alignment, counted from one.

::: table align="center" title="Candidate codon sites under episodic positive selection"

| Codon | $p$-value |
| ---: | ---: |
| 67 | 0.0525 |
| 72 | 0.0342 |
| 171 | 0.0750 |

:::

Only codon **72** also meets the stricter threshold of $p<0.05$. MEME's branch estimates suggest that `Obp19a` and `Obp56f` may contribute to the signal at codon 67, but this is exploratory evidence.

In contrast, aBSREL found **no significant evidence of episodic positive selection on any of the seven tested branches** after Holm–Bonferroni correction. This does not contradict the MEME results, because the methods test selection at different levels and used different alignments.

The three sites should be treated as candidates: the MEME p-values were not corrected for testing multiple sites, and the dataset contains only five sequences. Codon 171 is particularly uncertain because `Obp56f` and `Obp56g` have gaps at that position.
