## Presentation {#presentation}

Today I will explain how to find an optimal pairwise sequence alignment. I will begin with the scoring problem, derive the dynamic programming algorithm for global alignment, and demonstrate it with a small example. I will then explain local alignment and affine gap penalties.

A pairwise alignment places two sequences into correspondence while preserving the order of their residues. Each column contains either two residues or one residue and a gap.

The objective is to find the alignment with the highest score.

::: note Whiteboard

$$
A^* = \arg\max_{A} S(A)
$$

:::

Importantly, “optimal” means optimal under the chosen scoring scheme. It does not guarantee that the alignment represents the true evolutionary history.

---

The score combines contributions from aligned residue pairs with penalties for gaps. For DNA, we can use a simple match and mismatch scheme. For proteins, substitution matrices account for the fact that different amino acid substitutions have different evolutionary plausibilities.

For the example today, I will use a match score of plus two, a mismatch score of minus one, and a linear gap cost of two per gap character.

::: note Whiteboard

$$
S(A)=\sum s(x_i,y_j)-\sum c(k)
$$

$$
\text{match}=+2,\quad
\text{mismatch}=-1,\quad
d=2
$$

:::

We also need to choose the alignment objective. Global alignment aligns both complete sequences and is useful when they are comparable across their full lengths. Local alignment finds the highest-scoring pair of subsequences, for example a shared protein domain.

I will first consider global alignment using Needleman–Wunsch.

::: note Whiteboard

- Global → complete sequences
- Local → best subsequences
  :::

---

Let the two sequences be $X$ and $Y$, with lengths $m$ and $n$. I define $F(i,j)$ as the highest score for globally aligning the first $i$ residues of $X$ with the first $j$ residues of $Y$.

::: note Whiteboard

$$
F(i,j)=\text{best global score for }X_{1:i},Y_{1:j}
$$

:::

To derive the recurrence, consider the final column of an alignment.

First, $x_i$ may be aligned with $y_j$. We then take the optimal score for the two shorter prefixes and add their substitution score.

Second, $x_i$ may be aligned with a gap. We consume one residue from $X$, but none from $Y$, and subtract the gap cost.

Third, a gap may be aligned with $y_j$. This gives the corresponding horizontal move.

Taking the maximum covers all three possible endings.

::: note Whiteboard

$$
F(i,j)=\max
\begin{cases}
F(i-1,j-1)+s(x_i,y_j) & \text{diagonal}\\
F(i-1,j)-d & \text{up}\\
F(i,j-1)-d & \text{left}
\end{cases}
$$

:::
