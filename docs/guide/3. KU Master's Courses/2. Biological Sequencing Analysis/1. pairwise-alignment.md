Pairwise alignment is the foundation for most of the course: database search, read mapping, profiles and progressive multiple alignment all reuse its scoring and dynamic programming. The questions to be ready for are why log-odds scores, why DP is optimal, how local differs from global, and what affine gaps change.

## Objective and Scoring {#objective-and-scoring}

Pairwise alignment places corresponding residues of two sequences in columns, allowing gaps for insertions and deletions, and maximises a score. Residue order is preserved and gap–gap columns are not allowed. DNA is often scored with simple match/mismatch values, while proteins use substitution matrices. "Optimal" always means optimal under the chosen scoring scheme, not necessarily biologically correct.

$$
S(A) = \sum_{\text{residue pairs}} s(x_i, y_j) - \sum_{\text{gap runs}} g(k)
$$

Enumerating all alignments grows exponentially; dynamic programming (DP) avoids this by reusing optimal solutions for prefixes. The running example uses match $+2$, mismatch $-1$ and a linear gap cost $d = 2$.

The number of possible alignments explodes: two sequences of length 2 already have 13 global alignments (the Delannoy number $D(m,n) = \sum_k \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{k} 2^k$), and two sequences of length 100 have about $10^{75}$. The lecture splits the task into two sub-problems: **sampling** (generating candidate alignments) and **scoring** (ranking them). DP solves the search part exactly for a given scoring scheme.

## Global Alignment: Needleman–Wunsch {#global-alignment-needleman-wunsch}

Global alignment aligns both sequences end to end. For prefixes $x_{1..i}$ and $y_{1..j}$:

$$
F(i,j) = \max
\begin{cases}
F(i-1,j-1) + s(x_i, y_j) & \text{align residues} \\
F(i-1,j) - d & x_i \text{ against a gap} \\
F(i,j-1) - d & \text{a gap against } y_j
\end{cases}
$$

Initialise $F(0,0)=0$, $F(i,0) = -id$, $F(0,j) = -jd$, fill the table in dependency order, and trace back from $F(m,n)$ to $(0,0)$ along predecessors that attain the maximum. Ties can produce several co-optimal alignments.

**Whiteboard example.** $X = \text{ACG}$ (rows), $Y = \text{TC}$ (columns):

::: table align="center" title="Needleman–Wunsch matrix for ACG versus TC"

| | ∅ | T | C |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| ∅ | 0 | −2 | −4 |
| A | −2 | −1 | −3 |
| C | −4 | −3 | 1 |
| G | −6 | −5 | −1 |

:::

Traceback $(3,2) \to (2,2) \to (1,1) \to (0,0)$ gives

```text
A C G
T C -
```

with score $-1 + 2 - 2 = -1$.

## Why DP is Correct, and What It Costs {#why-dp-is-correct-and-what-it-costs}

If the last column of an optimal alignment is fixed, the remainder must be an optimal alignment of the corresponding prefixes; otherwise replacing it would improve the whole alignment. This is optimal substructure. There are $(m+1)(n+1)$ cells with $O(1)$ work each, so time and space are $O(mn)$. The score alone needs only two rows, i.e. $O(\min(m,n))$ space, but ordinary traceback then loses its history; recovering the alignment in linear space needs an additional method such as Hirschberg's algorithm.

## Local Alignment: Smith–Waterman {#local-alignment-smith-waterman}

$$
H(i,j) = \max\{0,\; H(i-1,j-1) + s(x_i,y_j),\; H(i-1,j) - d,\; H(i,j-1) - d\}
$$

with $H(i,0) = H(0,j) = 0$. The zero allows a restart, discarding negative-scoring prefixes. Traceback starts at the highest cell anywhere in the table and stops at zero, so poorly matching flanks are excluded. In the example above the local optimum is just C/C with score 2, whereas the global optimum scores $-1$. Neither mode is inherently better: the choice depends on the biological question (whole homologous genes versus a shared domain). Both run in $O(mn)$.

In the lecture example HEAGAWGHEE versus PAWHEAE (BLOSUM50, $d = 8$), the global score is 1, while the best local alignment `AWGHE` / `AW-HE` scores 28. The lecture example `GCATGCT` versus `GATTACA` (match $+1$, mismatch and gap $-1$) gives the global alignment `GCA-TGCT` / `G-ATTACA` with score 0.

::: table align="center" title="Needleman–Wunsch versus Smith–Waterman"

| | Needleman–Wunsch | Smith–Waterman |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Goal | Global | Local |
| Extra 0 option | No | Yes |
| Initialisation | $-id$, $-jd$ | 0 |
| Traceback starts | Bottom-right cell | Highest cell |
| Traceback ends | $(0,0)$ | First cell with score 0 |
| EMBOSS tool | `needle` | `water` |

:::

The zero in Smith–Waterman resets the *cumulative* score. Setting all negative *substitution scores* to zero would be wrong: mismatches would become free, the expected random score would turn positive, the local alignment would run to the ends of both sequences (effectively global), and E-value statistics would break down.

## Affine Gap Penalties {#affine-gap-penalties}

With a linear cost, one gap of length 5 costs the same as five gaps of length 1. Affine costs favour fewer, longer gaps, which is more realistic for indels. With $o > e > 0$:

$$
g(k) = kd \;\;(\text{linear}), \qquad g(k) = o + (k-1)e \;\;(\text{affine})
$$

Gotoh's formulation adds states that distinguish opening from extending:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(i,j) &= \max\{F(i-1,j) - o,\; I(i-1,j) - e\} \\
D(i,j) &= \max\{F(i,j-1) - o,\; D(i,j-1) - e\} \\
F(i,j) &= \max\{F(i-1,j-1) + s(x_i,y_j),\; I(i,j),\; D(i,j)\}
\end{aligned}
$$

$I$ ends in $x_i$/gap and $D$ in gap/$y_j$. For global alignment, boundary cells take the value $-g(i)$ or $-g(j)$ in the valid gap state, impossible states are $-\infty$, and $F(0,0) = 0$. A constant number of states keeps the complexity at $O(mn)$.

An affine cost corresponds to a geometric gap-length distribution, which is exactly what an insert state with a self-loop gives in a (profile) HMM. This is a useful bridge to [Topic 11](/docs/ku-bsa/profile-hmms/).

## Substitution Matrices {#substitution-matrices}

For a fixed ungapped alignment with independent columns, compare a related model $M$ (aligned-pair probabilities $p_{ab}$) with a random model $R$ (background frequencies $q_a$):

$$
\log\frac{P(X,Y \mid M)}{P(X,Y \mid R)} = \sum_i \log\frac{p_{x_i y_i}}{q_{x_i} q_{y_i}},
\qquad s(a,b) \propto \log\frac{p_{ab}}{q_a q_b}
$$

A positive score means the pair is enriched over chance, zero means equal, negative means depleted. Logs make scores additive, and conservative substitutions such as I/L/V can score positively without being identical.

::: table align="center" title="PAM versus BLOSUM"

| Matrix | Basis | Higher number means |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PAM | Close relatives, extrapolated evolutionary model | Greater distance |
| BLOSUM | Conserved local blocks | Smaller distance |

:::

BLOSUM is built from conserved, ungapped protein blocks: similar sequences are clustered to limit redundancy, weighted pairs are counted between clusters, and the frequencies are turned into scaled, rounded log-odds. BLOSUM62 refers to a 62% clustering threshold, not a minimum identity between every compared pair. BLOSUM80 suits close relatives, BLOSUM45 distant ones. PAM250 means 250 accepted substitutions per 100 residues, which allows repeated changes at the same site; it does not mean 250% observed difference.

::: warning Identity, similarity and homology
Identity is identical residues divided by alignment length. Similarity also counts conservative substitutions under a stated matrix. Homology means common ancestry and is a yes/no claim, not a percentage. A high score alone does not establish homology or shared function.
:::

Logs are used for three reasons: products become sums, so scores are additive and usable in DP; multiplying many small probabilities would underflow; and with base 2 the scores are in bits. The expected score of a random pair must be negative, $\sum_{a,b} q_a q_b\, s(a,b) < 0$, while some scores are positive; otherwise local alignment degenerates into global alignment.

Match scores also differ between residues: under BLOSUM62, W–W scores 11 but A–A only 4, because the reward $\log[p_{aa}/q_a^2]$ is large for rare, conserved residues. As a rule of thumb, use BLOSUM80 or PAM30 for close relatives, BLOSUM45 or PAM250 for distant ones, and BLOSUM62 as the default. The lecture also mentioned that the original BLOSUM code normalised only by the size of the second cluster; the corrected matrices are known as RBLOSUM.

## Limitations {#limitations}

- "Optimal" is relative to the scoring scheme; another matrix or other gap costs can change the optimum.
- Every position is scored by the same matrix, so position-specific conservation is ignored; profiles and profile HMMs address this.
- $O(mn)$ against a whole database or genome is too slow, which motivates BLAST/FASTA heuristics and indexing.
- Alignments are colinear, so rearrangements and domain shuffling are not captured.
- A high score is not homology, and homology is not identical function; significance needs statistics ([Topic 3](/docs/ku-bsa/score-statistics/)).
- Low-complexity and compositionally biased regions give spuriously high scores.

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Keyword Card {#keyword-card}

- **2 problems**: scoring + search; #alignments exponential (2×2 → 13; 100 → $10^{75}$)

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

Today I will talk about optimal pairwise sequence alignment. An alignment places two sequences on top of each other, keeping the order of the residues, and inserting gaps where we think an insertion or a deletion has happened. We do this to find equivalent — ideally homologous — positions between two sequences, for example to transfer knowledge about function from a well-studied protein to a new one, or as the core step in database searching and read mapping. I want to stress one distinction right away: homology means common ancestry, and it is a yes-or-no statement. Identity and similarity are percentages that we measure on an alignment, and we use them as evidence for homology.

There are two separate problems. The first is *scoring*: how do we decide that one alignment is better than another? The second is *searching*: the number of possible alignments grows exponentially — even two sequences of length two have thirteen different global alignments, and two sequences of length one hundred have around ten to the seventy-five. So we cannot enumerate; we need an algorithm that finds the best one efficiently.

Let me start with scoring. We compare two models for an aligned pair of sequences. In the random model, the two letters in a column are independent and each occurs with its background frequency, so the probability of a column with *a* and *b* is $q_a q_b$. In the match model, the two letters are related, and the pair occurs with the joint probability $p_{ab}$ that we estimate from alignments of truly related sequences. The ratio of these two probabilities tells us how much more likely the alignment is if the sequences are related. If we take the logarithm, the product over columns becomes a sum, so the score of an alignment is just the sum of the column scores.

$$
S=\sum_i s(x_i,y_i),\qquad s(a,b)=\log\frac{p_{ab}}{q_aq_b}
$$

Logs have three advantages: scores become additive, which is exactly what dynamic programming needs; we avoid multiplying many tiny probabilities, which would underflow; and with log base two the scores are in bits. A positive score means the pair is seen more often in related proteins than by chance — that can also be a mismatch, like isoleucine and valine. For proteins we use BLOSUM or PAM matrices. BLOSUM is estimated from conserved, ungapped blocks of protein families, with sequences clustered at a given identity to avoid over-counting; BLOSUM62 is the default, a higher number means more closely related sequences. PAM is extrapolated from very close relatives, and there a higher number means larger evolutionary distance. One important property: the expected score of two random residues must be negative, otherwise a local alignment would just keep growing.

Now the algorithm: dynamic programming. The idea is to solve small problems optimally, store their solutions and build larger solutions from them. Define $F(i,j)$ as the best score of aligning the first $i$ letters of $x$ with the first $j$ letters of $y$. The last column of such an alignment can only be one of three things: $x_i$ aligned to $y_j$, $x_i$ aligned to a gap, or a gap aligned to $y_j$. So

$$
F(i,j)=\max\begin{cases}F(i-1,j-1)+s(x_i,y_j)\\ F(i-1,j)-d\\ F(i,j-1)-d\end{cases}
$$

and this is correct because of optimal substructure: once the last column is fixed, the rest must be an optimal alignment of the shorter prefixes, otherwise we could improve the whole alignment. For a global alignment — Needleman–Wunsch — we initialise $F(0,0)=0$ and the first row and column with accumulated gap penalties, $-id$ and $-jd$.

Let me do a small example: $x$ = ACG, $y$ = TC, match plus two, mismatch minus one, gap minus two.

For example, the cell for C against C: from the diagonal we get −1 plus 2, which is 1; from above or the left we would get much less, so it is 1. The final score in the bottom-right corner is −1. Then we trace back from the bottom-right to the top-left, following the cells that produced the maximum: G against a gap, C against C, A against T. That gives `ACG` over `TC-` with score −1. If there are ties, there are several co-optimal alignments.

For local alignment — Smith–Waterman — we change two things. We add a fourth option, zero, to the maximum, which means: if the best alignment ending here has a negative score, it is better to start a new alignment here. Consequently the first row and column are zero, and the traceback starts at the highest cell anywhere in the matrix and stops when we reach a zero. In the same example the local optimum is just C with C, with score 2. In the lecture example, HEAGAWGHEE against PAWHEAE with BLOSUM50, the global score is 1 while the best local alignment, AWGHE against AW-HE, scores 28. Which one to use is a biological question: global for sequences that are related over their full length, local for shared domains, or when searching a database where the hit has unrelated flanks.

Next, gaps. With a linear penalty every gap position costs the same, so five separate one-residue gaps cost the same as one gap of five. Biologically one indel event often covers several residues, so we prefer affine gaps: a large opening cost and a smaller extension cost.

$$
g(k)=d_{open}+(k-1)\,d_{extend}
$$

To implement this we need to remember whether we are already in a gap, so we keep three matrices: one for alignments ending in a match, one ending in a gap in $x$, one ending in a gap in $y$. The complexity is still $O(mn)$, just with a factor three.

Finally, complexity and limitations. We fill $m$ times $n$ cells with constant work per cell, so time and memory are $O(mn)$. If we only need the score we can keep two rows. That is fine for two proteins, but doing this against a whole database, or for millions of sequencing reads, is too slow — which is why BLAST and indexing methods exist. Also, "optimal" only means optimal under the scoring scheme; another matrix or other gap penalties can give another alignment, scores treat every position the same way, and a high score alone is not proof of homology — for that we need statistics like the E-value. *If we have a family of sequences we can do better with position-specific scores — profiles and profile HMMs — and the affine gap penalty reappears there as an insert state with a self-loop.* That is how optimal pairwise alignment underlies most of the rest of the course.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- ★ Why are the calculations easier with logs?

  Because the alignment probability under the match/random models is a product over columns; taking logs turns the product into a sum, so each cell of the DP matrix just adds one column score — that additivity is exactly what dynamic programming needs. Logs also avoid numerical underflow from multiplying many probabilities < 1, and in base 2 the scores are bits, which are interpretable.

- ★ In the substitution matrix, could you just change all negative numbers to zero?

  No. Negative entries encode that a pair is less frequent in related sequences than by chance. If you set them to zero, mismatches become free, the expected score of a random pair becomes positive, and the best local alignment of two unrelated sequences would extend over their whole length — local alignment degenerates into global alignment and the E-value statistics (which require a negative expected score) break down. The "0" in Smith–Waterman is different: it truncates the *cumulative* score of an alignment (start a new alignment), not individual pair scores.

- ★ What happens with affine gaps? (And can you do the alignment on the board?)

  Affine gaps $g(k)=d_o+(k-1)d_e$ make opening expensive and extending cheap, so the optimum prefers one long gap over several short ones — matching the biology that one indel event often spans several residues. Algorithmically you must remember whether you are already in a gap, so you use three matrices (match, gap in x, gap in y) — Gotoh's algorithm — still $O(mn)$. Be ready to fill the ACG/TC matrix (match +2, mismatch −1, $d=2$; result −1, `ACG/TC-`) and explain each cell.

- Why does dynamic programming give the optimal alignment?

  Optimal substructure: the last column is either a pair, x-vs-gap or gap-vs-y; whatever it is, the remaining part must be an optimal alignment of the corresponding prefixes, otherwise replacing it would improve the total. Overlapping subproblems are stored in the matrix, so each is solved once. Since we take the max over all three possibilities at every cell, no alignment is missed.

- How many possible alignments are there?

  Exponentially many. Two length-2 sequences already have 13 global alignments (Delannoy number); for length 100 about $10^{75}$; Durbin gives $\approx 2^{2n}/\sqrt{\pi n}$ for two length-$n$ sequences. Hence DP rather than enumeration.

- Global vs local — when do you use which?

  Global (Needleman–Wunsch) when the sequences are homologous over their entire length and of similar length (orthologs). Local (Smith–Waterman) when only part is shared (a domain, a motif), or in database search where the hit sits inside unrelated flanking sequence. Differences: SW adds a 0 option, initialises with 0, starts traceback at the matrix maximum and stops at 0.

- What is the time and memory complexity? Can you reduce memory?

  $O(mn)$ time and memory. For the score alone you need only the previous row → $O(\min(m,n))$ memory; to also recover the alignment in linear space, use Hirschberg's divide-and-conquer.

- How is BLOSUM62 constructed, and what does the 62 mean?

  From the BLOCKS database of conserved, ungapped regions of protein families. Sequences within a block that are ≥ 62% identical are clustered and weighted as one, to avoid over-representing near-identical sequences; then pair frequencies $p_{ab}$ between clusters and background $q_a$ are computed and $s=\frac1\lambda\log\frac{p_{ab}}{q_aq_b}$, scaled and rounded. 62 is the clustering threshold, not a minimum identity of every pair. (Lecture: the original code had a normalisation bug — only cluster 2 size — corrected RBLOSUM.)

- BLOSUM vs PAM; which for distant proteins?

  PAM: global alignments of close relatives (≥ 85% identity), PAM1 = 1% accepted mutations, higher PAMs extrapolated by matrix powers; larger number = more distant. BLOSUM: local blocks, directly observed at each level; larger number = closer. Distant proteins: BLOSUM45 or PAM250; close: BLOSUM80 or PAM30. BLOSUM62 is the general default.

- Can a mismatch have a positive score? Can a match have a low score?

  Yes — conservative substitutions (I↔V, K↔R) occur more often in related proteins than by chance, so $s>0$. Identity scores also differ: W–W (rare, conserved) scores 11–15, while A–A only ~4–5, because the reward for a match is high when the residue is rare in the background.

- What does a negative score for an aligned pair mean? Does it mean the sequences are unrelated?

  The pair is less frequent in related sequences than expected by chance. One unfavourable column can sit inside a good overall alignment; only the total (and its statistics) matters.

- Identity vs similarity vs homology?

  Identity: fraction of identical columns. Similarity: also counts positive-scoring (conservative) substitutions, so ≥ identity, and depends on the matrix. Homology: common ancestry — qualitative; we never say "70% homologous".

- Why must the expected score of random alignment be negative?

  Otherwise long random alignments accumulate positive score and local alignment cannot distinguish real similarity — it would run to the ends. It is also required for Karlin–Altschul statistics.

- What are the limitations of optimal pairwise alignment?

  Optimal only under the chosen parameters; position-independent scoring; can't capture rearrangements/domain shuffling (only colinear alignments); slow for databases; significance needs statistics; low-complexity regions give spurious scores.

:::
