## What a Family Alignment Is Used For {#purpose}

Columns ideally represent shared ancestral residues or equivalent structural positions; these two goals need not coincide.

- **Uses**: remote homology, family definition, functional sites, phylogeny, profiles and co-evolution.
- **Goals for a good alignment**: align key residues and structural cores; keep alignments compact and indels coherent. A high score does not guarantee biological correctness.
- **Contact prediction**: rank inferred residue couplings to construct a contact map, and use likely contacts as restraints in 3D modelling, analogous to experimental distance restraints. The lecture connects co-evolutionary information with AlphaFold2's integration of sequence and structural information.
- **Other uses**: functional sites, interactions, variant effects and sequence design. Generated sequences should be evaluated on features excluded from fitting; plausible sequences do not establish function.

## Reading an Alignment and Practical Decisions {#reading-an-alignment}

A conserved column suggests a constrained residue (active site, structural core); gap-rich regions often correspond to loops and are the least reliable. Before interpreting:

- check that known key residues (e.g. catalytic residues) are aligned in the same column;
- check that gaps sit in loops rather than in the middle of helices;
- check that redundant, near-identical sequences do not dominate; thin or down-weight them.

Early errors in a progressive alignment are carried into later merges, so inspect and refine. If all sequences are close relatives, everything looks conserved simply for lack of time to diverge.

## Progressive Alignment and Refinement {#progressive-alignment}

::: steps

1. Estimate pairwise distances (pairwise alignments or fast $k$-mer comparisons).
2. Build a **guide tree**, e.g. UPGMA or neighbour joining.
3. Merge from leaves to root: sequence–sequence, sequence–profile, then profile–profile DP. Similar sequences are aligned first; existing within-group correspondences are preserved.

:::

With linear SP scoring, the within-group scores are fixed, leaving the cross-group scores to optimise; the gap–gap score is zero. A profile column pair scores

$$
\sum_{a,b} f_i(a)\, g_j(b)\, s(a,b)
$$

with column frequencies $f_i$ and $g_j$. UPGMA merges the closest clusters and averages distances.

==The guide tree is a merge order, not necessarily a phylogeny.== Early errors persist ("once a gap, always a gap"); iterative refinement splits and realigns groups, without guaranteeing a global optimum. MUSCLE uses $k$-mer distances, progressive alignment and refinement. Other examples are Clustal and MAFFT.

## Co-evolution: From Conservation to Candidate Contacts {#co-evolution}

Conservation concerns one column; co-evolution concerns joint variation between columns. Interacting residues may undergo **compensatory substitutions** that preserve charge, packing or function. Example: an interacting K/E pair changes to E/K; either single change can disrupt the interaction, while the double change restores compatibility.

### Why Correlation Is Not Contact {#why-correlation-is-not-contact}

- **Indirect coupling**: if A interacts with B and B with C, A and C may correlate without a direct interaction.
- **Shared ancestry / sampling bias**: related sequences are not independent observations, and overrepresented clades can dominate the counts.
- **Data quality**: misalignment, gaps and limited sequence diversity distort frequencies. Functional constraints can also link physically distant sites, and strong couplings may reflect contacts between subunits or alternative conformations.

> **Say:** Correlation can be indirect: A and C may correlate because both depend on B. We therefore model all positions jointly to estimate direct couplings.

Use diverse homologues, check the alignment and weight sequences; weighting cannot remove all phylogenetic confounding.

## Entropy and Sum-of-Pairs Scores {#scoring-entropy-and-sum-of-pairs}

For an alignment $M$ with columns $M_i$, a common objective is $S(M) = G(M) + \sum_i S(M_i)$, where $G$ is the gap contribution. This additive form ignores dependencies between columns.

**Minimum entropy**: $c_i(a)$ counts residue $a$, $n_i = \sum_a c_i(a)$ and $p_i(a) = c_i(a)/n_i$:

$$
H_i = -\sum_a p_i(a) \log_2 p_i(a), \qquad -\log_2 P(M_i) = -\sum_a c_i(a) \log_2 p_i(a) = n_i H_i.
$$

Conserved columns have $H_i = 0$; mixed columns have higher entropy ($0 \log 0 = 0$). Gaps must be penalised to prevent artificial conservation.

**Sum of pairs** (SP): sum substitution scores (PAM/BLOSUM) over all sequence pairs in each column, counting each pair once, plus the gap contribution. It is a practical objective, not a full phylogenetic model.

**Whiteboard example.** ACG, ACT, AGT, ACG; four sequences give $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs per column. With match 1 / mismatch 0:

::: table align="center" title="Entropy and SP scores for the toy MSA"

| Column composition | $H_i$ (bits) | $n_i H_i$ | SP |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 4 A | 0 | 0 | 6 |
| 3 C, 1 G | 0.811 | 3.245 | 3 |
| 2 G, 2 T | 1 | 4 | 2 |

:::

Both SP and minimum entropy treat the sequences as independent observations: with many close relatives the same substitution is counted many times, and the phylogeny is ignored. Sequence weighting is the usual remedy.

## Why Exact Alignment Is Infeasible {#why-exact-msa-is-infeasible}

Exact DP over $N$ sequences of length $L$ needs $(L+1)^N$ cells with $2^N - 1$ predecessor moves each. The exponential cost motivates heuristics; exact alignment is feasible only for a handful of short sequences.

## Measuring Co-variation: Mutual Information {#mutual-information}

Let $p_i(a)$ be a marginal frequency and $p_{ij}(a,b)$ a joint frequency. Independence predicts $p_{ij}(a,b) = p_i(a)\, p_j(b)$.

$$
C_{ij}(a,b) = p_{ij}(a,b) - p_i(a)\, p_j(b), \qquad
MI(i,j) = \sum_{a,b} p_{ij}(a,b) \log_2 \frac{p_{ij}(a,b)}{p_i(a)\, p_j(b)}.
$$

MI measures how much observing one position tells us about the other. Independent columns give zero MI; ==fully conserved columns carry no co-evolutionary signal==.

**Toy calculation**: half the sequences contain K/E, half E/K. Both marginals are $(1/2, 1/2)$, but only two of the four combinations occur. Each observed pair has $C = 1/2 - 1/4 = 1/4$, and $MI = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \log_2 2 = 1$ bit.

## Global Coupling Model and Direct Coupling Analysis {#global-coupling-models}

A pairwise statistical model (Potts model) represents position preferences and couplings together:

$$
P(\mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\!\left[\sum_i h_i(a_i) + \sum_{i<j} J_{ij}(a_i, a_j)\right].
$$

$h_i$ are single-position preferences, $J_{ij}$ pairwise couplings, and $Z$ the normalisation over all sequences. The model is fitted to the observed single- and pair-site frequencies. **Direct coupling analysis** (DCA) seeks couplings that explain the observed correlations jointly, which helps separate direct from indirect effects: a correlation explained through a chain of other couplings gets a small direct coupling.

In the lecture, covariance-matrix inversion is one approximate route to the couplings (mean-field). Strong inferred couplings suggest candidate contacts, not proof of physical interaction.

Many parameters require many diverse sequences; pseudocounts and regularisation help with sparse observations. The exact $Z$ sums over $q^L$ sequences ($q = 20$, or 21 including gaps), making exact inference impractical for typical proteins, so approximate inference is required.

::: table align="center" title="Profile versus coupling models"

| Model | What it captures |
| :--- | :--- |
| PSSM | Independent position preferences |
| Profile HMM | Adds ordered match/insert/delete paths |
| Coupling (Potts) model | Adds pairwise dependencies between positions |

:::

All depend on alignment quality and diversity.

Uses of an MSA → reading columns → guide tree and progressive merges → compensatory pair → indirect correlation → support: entropy/SP example → exponential DP → MI → global couplings and contact map.

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

**What it is for.** Suppose I have collected a family of homologous proteins and I want to know which residues matter. A multiple sequence alignment places the equivalent residues of all sequences in the same column. Once I have it, I can read off a lot: columns that are conserved across the family point to residues under functional or structural constraint, such as an active site; the pattern of differences is the input for a phylogenetic tree; the column frequencies give a profile or profile HMM for finding more family members; and pairs of columns that change together can reveal residues that are in contact in the three-dimensional structure. So the alignment is rarely the end result; it is the starting point for almost every family-level analysis.

**What a good alignment is.** I need an objective. One is the sum of pairs score: for each column I add the substitution scores of all pairs of residues in it, and I add gap penalties. Another is minimum entropy: a column in which all sequences have the same residue has entropy zero, a mixed column has high entropy, and a good alignment has low total entropy. Both treat columns independently, and both are only models: the alignment with the best score is not guaranteed to be the biologically correct one.

**Why the exact solution is not used.** I could extend dynamic programming from two sequences to $N$: the table then has $N$ dimensions with about $L^N$ cells, and each cell has $2^N - 1$ predecessors. For ten proteins of length three hundred this is completely infeasible. So practical programs use a heuristic.

**Progressive alignment.** This is the standard heuristic, used by programs of the Clustal type. Step one: compute a distance between every pair of sequences, from pairwise alignments or from fast word counts. Step two: build a guide tree from these distances, for example with UPGMA or neighbour joining. Step three: follow the tree from the leaves to the root. The two most similar sequences are aligned first; then a sequence is aligned to that alignment, or two alignments are aligned to each other, treating each existing alignment as a profile, with the score of a column pair being the average substitution score between them. The most reliable alignments are made first, when the sequences are similar, and the difficult, distant ones last.

**The weakness, and what I do about it.** Decisions made early are never revisited: a gap that was placed when two sequences were aligned stays there in all later steps, the rule known as once a gap, always a gap. So an early error propagates. Remedies are iterative refinement, where a sequence or group is removed and realigned to the rest, and consistency-based scoring. As a user I check the result: are known key residues, for example the catalytic residues, in the same column; are the gaps concentrated in loops and not in the middle of helices; is the family dominated by many nearly identical sequences that should be thinned or down-weighted.

**From conservation to co-evolution.** Conservation looks at one column at a time. Co-evolution looks at two columns together. The idea is this: if two residues touch each other in the folded protein, a mutation in one may be harmful unless the partner changes too. A positively charged lysine facing a negatively charged glutamate may become glutamate facing lysine. Over evolution the two columns then vary in a coordinated way. I can measure this with mutual information, which asks how much knowing the residue in one column tells me about the residue in the other. In the toy case where half the sequences have lysine and glutamate and the other half glutamate and lysine, each column alone looks completely variable, but together they carry one full bit of mutual information.

**Why correlation is not yet contact.** There are three problems. The first is indirect coupling: if A touches B and B touches C, then A and C are also correlated although they are not in contact. The second is phylogeny: the sequences are related by a tree, so two columns can agree simply because both mutations happened once in the ancestor of a large clade. The third is sampling: too few or too similar sequences give noisy estimates. Direct coupling analysis addresses the first problem. Instead of looking at each pair in isolation, it fits one global model to the whole alignment, with a parameter for the preference of each position and a coupling parameter for each pair of positions, and asks which couplings are needed to explain all observed correlations at once. A correlation that can be explained through a chain of other couplings gets a small direct coupling.

**How the result is used.** I rank the pairs of positions by their coupling strength. The top pairs are predicted contacts, which I can draw as a contact map and use as distance restraints to build or check a three-dimensional model. This is the same kind of information that modern structure prediction such as AlphaFold extracts from multiple sequence alignments. The requirements are a deep and diverse alignment, many sequences that are not near-duplicates, and a correct alignment, because a misaligned column destroys the signal. And a strong coupling is a prediction: it can also reflect contacts between subunits, alternative conformations or a functional link without physical contact.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- Why not compute the optimal multiple alignment by dynamic programming?

  The table has one dimension per sequence, so time and memory grow exponentially with the number of sequences, roughly $L^N$ cells times $2^N$ moves. It is feasible only for a handful of short sequences.

- What is the role of the guide tree? Is it the phylogeny?

  It only determines the order in which sequences and groups are merged, so that similar sequences are aligned first. It is built quickly from approximate distances and is not meant as a reliable phylogeny; a proper tree is inferred afterwards from the finished alignment.

- What is the main weakness of progressive alignment?

  It is greedy. Gaps and pairings introduced in an early merge are fixed and cannot be corrected when later sequences show they were wrong. Iterative refinement and consistency scoring reduce the problem but do not remove it.

- ★ What is profile–profile alignment?

  Aligning two alignments to each other with ordinary pairwise DP, where each position is a column (frequency vector) rather than a single letter. The column-pair score is e.g. $\sum_{a,b} f_i(a) g_j(b) s(a,b)$, gaps are inserted as whole columns, and the existing within-profile alignment is kept. It is used at the internal nodes of the guide tree and for sensitive homology detection (HHpred aligns profile HMMs).

- ★ Sum of pairs and minimum entropy treat the sequences as … what? Why is that a problem for phylogeny?

  As independent observations. But sequences are related through a phylogeny: closely related sequences share residues because of common descent, not because of constraint, and a cluster of near-identical sequences dominates the score because a substitution on one branch is counted many times. Remedies are sequence weighting or phylogeny-aware scoring.

- ★ What effect does a bad MSA have on the inferred phylogeny?

  Phylogenetic inference assumes each column contains positionally homologous residues. Misalignment places non-homologous residues together, creating false substitutions (long branches) or false identities, which can give wrong topologies with high apparent support; misplaced gaps create spurious indel signals. Because progressive alignment follows a guide tree, a tree inferred from it can also be biased towards that guide tree. Remedies: better aligners, trimming unreliable columns, or co-estimating alignment and tree.

- How do you recognise a functionally important residue in an alignment, and what could mislead you?

  By strong conservation across diverse members of the family, for example low entropy in the column. I can be misled if the sequences are all close relatives, because then everything looks conserved simply for lack of time to diverge, or if the column is misaligned.

- A fully conserved column: how much co-evolution signal does it carry?

  None. Co-evolution needs variation: mutual information with any other column is zero if the column never changes. Conservation and co-evolution are complementary signals.

- Two positions have high mutual information. Are they in contact?

  Not necessarily. The correlation may be indirect through a third residue, it may come from the phylogenetic relationship of the sequences, or from too few sequences. Direct coupling analysis, sequence weighting and corrections for background correlation are used to separate direct from indirect effects, and the top-ranked pairs are still predictions.

- What is the difference between a profile and a coupling model?

  A profile, or a profile HMM, describes each position independently: which residues are preferred there. A coupling model adds a term for every pair of positions, so it can express that a residue at one position favours a particular residue at another. The first captures conservation, the second also co-variation.

:::
