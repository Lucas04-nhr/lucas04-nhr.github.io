Database search applies local alignment at scale. The core of this topic is the seed-and-extend idea, how BLAST and BLAT differ in what they index, and the trade-off between sensitivity and speed controlled by word length and threshold.

## The Search Problem and the Seed Principle {#the-search-problem-and-the-seed-principle}

Database searching asks which sequences contain a region similar to the query. Smith–Waterman over the whole database costs $O(lL)$ for query length $l$ and total database length $L$, which is accurate but too slow at scale. Heuristics restrict expensive alignment to promising candidates, at the price of possibly missing true matches:

```text
generate words → find seeds → extend → evaluate
```

The pigeonhole argument justifies exact seeds: with $n$ aligned positions and at most $d$ mismatches, split the alignment into $d+1$ blocks; at least one block is mismatch-free and has length at least

$$
k = \left\lfloor \frac{n}{d+1} \right\rfloor.
$$

This guarantee assumes mismatches only, not arbitrary indels.

The biological motivation is to find homologues of a query in order to transfer functional or structural knowledge. Distant homologues may share only 20–30% identity; protein sequence is more conserved than DNA (because of the degenerate code), structure more than sequence, and function more than structure.

## Fast Lookup and the Seed-length Trade-off {#fast-lookup-and-the-seed-length-trade-off}

Instead of comparing every query word with every database position, preprocess the query words into a lookup structure and scan the database once. For fixed $k$ the scan is roughly $O(L)$ plus preprocessing, hit reporting and extension, so total work still depends on database size. A hash table gives expected constant-time lookup with controlled load and collision handling; a search tree is an alternative.

Short words are sensitive but produce many chance hits; long words reduce random matches but miss divergent regions. Low-complexity masking suppresses repetitive, uninformative seeds, and a database index trades memory for speed when many queries are run.

## BLAST Step by Step {#blast-step-by-step}

BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool):

1. Mask low-complexity regions and generate overlapping query words.
2. For proteins, generate neighbourhood words, score them against the query word with a matrix such as BLOSUM62, and keep those above a threshold.
3. Scan the database for exact occurrences of retained words.
4. Extend promising hits into high-scoring segment pairs (HSPs), process them further, and report alignments with significance.

A database word therefore need not equal the original query word; it exactly matches a retained neighbour. Lowering the word-score threshold usually increases sensitivity and computation.

**Whiteboard example.** For query word PQG, the neighbour PEG scores $7 + 2 + 6 = 15$ under BLOSUM62, so Q/E is accepted if 15 meets the threshold. Extending the seed:

::: table align="center" title="Ungapped extension of the PQG/PEG seed"

| | Flank | | | Seed | | | Flank |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Query | R | P | P | Q | G | L | F |
| Database | D | P | P | E | G | V | V |
| Score | −2 | 7 | 7 | 2 | 6 | 1 | −1 |

:::

The five-residue segment PPQGL/PPEGV scores 23 and is an HSP in this ungapped example. The seed is only a starting point: extension tests whether the surrounding sequence supports a longer high-scoring region, and a negative substitution need not stop extension immediately. Later steps can produce gapped local alignments, and one sequence pair may contain several separately scored local regions.

Ungapped extension stops when the running score drops more than $X$ below the best score seen so far (X-drop). Classic word sizes are $k = 3$ for proteins (with e.g. $T = 11$ under BLOSUM62) and $k = 11$ for blastn (28 for megablast).

Gapped BLAST (1997) uses the **two-hit method**: an extension is triggered only by two non-overlapping hits on the same diagonal within a window $A$ of about 40 residues. This allows a lower $T$ (more sensitive) while reducing the number of costly extensions. Lowering $T$ or $k$ gives more seeds, higher sensitivity and slower searches; raising them is faster but may miss distant homologues.

In the exercise, Taq polymerase was **not** found in *E. coli* with blastn but **was** found with tblastn. Protein-level comparison is more sensitive because synonymous changes alter DNA but not protein, and substitution matrices reward conservative replacements. Changing the blastn reward/penalty from 2/−3 to 1/−4 split one hit into two shorter, higher-identity HSPs, because local alignment stops extending into mismatch-rich regions. ## Significance {#significance}

A large database offers many opportunities for chance matches. The E-value is the expected number of chance matches scoring at least $S$:

$$
E(S) = Kmn\,e^{-\lambda S}, \qquad P(\text{at least one}) \approx 1 - e^{-E(S)}
$$

Higher scores give exponentially smaller E-values; a tenfold larger database gives roughly a tenfold larger E-value. The E-value is an expected count, can exceed 1, and is not the probability that two sequences are unrelated. For small $E$, $P \approx E$. Coverage, length, identity and biological context should be inspected alongside it.

## BLAT: Index the Genome Instead of the Query {#blat-index-the-genome-instead-of-the-query}

BLAT (BLAST-Like Alignment Tool) keeps a hash of non-overlapping target words in memory, omitting very common repeats. Overlapping query words are looked up, compatible hits are grouped and then extended. Typical word sizes in the course were 11-mers for DNA and 4–5-mers for protein. Because the target index is reused for many queries, BLAT suits high-identity mapping of transcripts or sequences to a genome, while BLAST supports broader similarity searches.

## Choosing the Word Length {#choosing-the-word-length}

Let $q$ be identity per position, $w$ the number of independent word opportunities, and $p = q^k$. The number of matching words is $X \sim \text{Binomial}(w, p)$:

$$
P(X=0) = (1-p)^w, \qquad P(X=1) = wp(1-p)^{w-1}
$$

One-hit detection is $1 - P(X=0)$; requiring two hits gives $1 - P(X=0) - P(X=1)$. Increasing $k$ lowers $q^k$: fewer random hits but more missed true matches. These are approximations because overlapping words are dependent.

For random sequence over an alphabet of size $M$, a word matches with probability $M^{-k}$, so for target length $L$ and query length $l$:

$$
E(\text{random seed pairs}) \approx \frac{L}{k}(l - k + 1)M^{-k}
$$

Requiring two hits close together on the same diagonal ($j - i$ constant for ungapped matches) makes random coincidences far rarer. With $N_2$ eligible pairs of non-overlapping seed comparisons, independence gives $E_2 \approx N_2 M^{-2k}$; the eligible pairs must be counted first rather than squaring the one-hit expectation. This lets a shorter $k$ recover sensitivity while controlling extension work. Indels shift the diagonal and are handled by chaining or gapped alignment.

FASTA follows a related route: find exact word hits ($k = 1$–2 for proteins, 4–6 for DNA), identify high-scoring diagonals, rescore and join nearby regions, then run Smith–Waterman in selected windows. Final optimal refinement cannot rescue regions discarded during heuristic filtering.

## Choosing a Method {#choosing-a-method}

::: table align="center" title="Search methods compared"

| Method | Cost | Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Smith–Waterman (SSEARCH) | $O(lL)$ | Maximal sensitivity, small databases |
| FASTA | Heuristic, diagonal-based | Historical predecessor |
| BLAST | About $O(L)$ scan plus extensions (query indexed) | Proteins, distant homologues, any database |
| BLAT | Hash lookups per query word (genome indexed) | Near-identical DNA/mRNA to a genome, many queries |
| BWA/Bowtie (FM-index) | $O(\text{read length})$ per exact seed | Millions of short reads ([Topic 5](/docs/ku-bsa/ngs-and-bwt/)) |

:::

The common limitation of the heuristics is that no seed means no hit: a true homologue without a word above $T$ is never aligned, and the final optimal step cannot rescue it. Results are only as good as the scoring scheme, and low-complexity regions and repeats must be masked.

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

My topic is searching sequence databases with pairwise alignment methods, mainly BLAST and BLAT. The typical biological question is: I have a new protein, which known proteins are homologous to it? If I find homologs with known function or structure, I can transfer that knowledge. Distant homologs may share only 20 to 30 percent identity, so the search has to be sensitive, and databases contain millions of sequences, so it also has to be fast.

The exact method would be Smith–Waterman local alignment of the query against every database sequence. That costs time proportional to the query length times the total database length, $O(lL)$ — accurate but too slow, and Moore's law does not save us because databases grow faster than computers. So BLAST and BLAT are heuristics: they decide quickly where an alignment is likely, and only do the expensive work there. The price is that they can miss true hits.

The lecture built this from four ideas. First, break a long approximate match into short exact matches. If two strings of length $n$ match with at most $d$ mismatches, then by the pigeonhole principle they must share an exact word of length $n$ divided by $d$ plus one: cut the match into $d+1$ blocks, and at most $d$ of them contain a mismatch.

$$
k=\left\lfloor \frac{n}{d+1}\right\rfloor
$$

Exact words can be looked up extremely fast with a hash table. Second, only use words that score highly — common, low-complexity words are poor starting points, so BLAST masks low-complexity regions. Third — and this is the clever part for proteins — the seeds do not need to be exact matches to the query. Fourth, seeds are extended into longer alignments.

Let me walk through BLAST, which stands for Basic Local Alignment Search Tool. Step one: filter low-complexity regions in the query. Step two: cut the query into overlapping words of length $k$, for proteins usually three. Step three: for each word, generate all neighbourhood words — all three-letter words that score at least a threshold $T$ against the query word with a substitution matrix like BLOSUM62.

For example, for the query word PQG, the word PEG scores 7 plus 2 plus 6, which is 15 — glutamine and glutamate are similar — so PEG is kept. Step four: put these words in a lookup table, and step five: scan the database for exact occurrences of any of them. Note that a hit in the database is an exact match to a neighbourhood word, not necessarily to the original query word. So we use fast exact matching but still capture biologically similar sequences.

Step six: extend each hit in both directions without gaps, summing substitution scores, as long as the score does not drop too far below the best score seen so far — this is the X-drop criterion; a single negative column does not stop the extension.

For example, extending the PQG–PEG seed in both directions gives the segment PPQGL against PPEGV with a total score of 23.

The result is a high-scoring segment pair, an HSP. Step seven: keep HSPs above a cutoff, do a gapped extension, and compute an E-value, which tells us how many hits with this score we would expect by chance in a database of this size. Finally BLAST reports the local alignments, their scores and E-values; one database sequence can give several separate HSPs. The modern version uses a two-hit rule: only extend if there are two non-overlapping hits on the same diagonal within about 40 residues. That allows a lower threshold $T$ — more sensitive — while keeping the number of costly extensions down.

The trade-off is controlled by $k$ and $T$: a lower threshold gives more seeds, more sensitivity, and more time. In the exercise we saw that a DNA search for Taq polymerase in *E. coli* with blastn found nothing, while tblastn, which compares at the protein level in all six reading frames, found the *E. coli* polymerase: protein sequences are more conserved than DNA because of the degenerate genetic code, and substitution matrices capture conservative changes.

BLAT, the BLAST-Like Alignment Tool, was made for genomes. The key difference: BLAST indexes the query and scans the database; BLAT builds a hash table of the database — the genome — once, and keeps it in memory. It indexes non-overlapping $k$-mers of the genome, 11-mers for DNA and 4- to 5-mers for protein, excluding very common repeats. Then for a query it looks up all overlapping $k$-mers, and gets hits immediately. It is meant for near-identical matches, for example mapping mRNA to the genome to see which genes are active, where differences come mostly from sequencing errors.

How do we choose $k$? Let $q$ be the identity between query and true match, and $w$ the number of words in the match. A word matches perfectly with probability $q^k$, so we miss one word with probability $1-q^k$, and miss the whole hit with probability $(1-q^k)^w$. On the other hand, a random word matches with probability one over $M$ to the $k$, where $M$ is the alphabet size, and the expected number of random hits is roughly $L/k$ times $l$ times $M^{-k}$.

$$
P(\text{word hit})=q^k,\quad P(\text{miss})=(1-q^k)^w,\quad P(\text{random word})=M^{-k},\quad \#\text{random hits}\approx\frac{L}{k}\,l\,M^{-k}
$$

So short words give too many random hits and long words miss true matches. The solution, like in FASTA, is to require two hits close to each other on the same diagonal: the probability of missing increases a little — we now also miss when exactly one word matches, $w\,q^k(1-q^k)^{w-1}$ — but random pairs on the same diagonal are very rare, so we can afford a shorter $k$.

To summarise when to use what: BLAST for proteins and for distant homologs, because of the neighbourhood words and substitution matrices; BLAT for fast, near-identical DNA searches against a genome with many queries; Smith–Waterman when you need maximal sensitivity on a small database; and for millions of short reads, BWT-based mappers. The limitations: heuristics can miss true hits — no seed, no hit — and the reported significance must be judged with E-values, coverage and biological context, which is my link to score statistics.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: collapse

- ★ With seed-and-extend, if there are multiple seeds on one diagonal, is there any distance requirement?

  Yes. In the two-hit method of gapped BLAST, an extension is triggered only by two non-overlapping word hits on the same diagonal within a window $A$ (default ~40 residues). Same diagonal means the offset $j-i$ is equal, i.e. they are consistent with one ungapped alignment; the distance limit ensures they could belong to the same HSP. In BLAT the hits must also be near each other (and gaps/introns are allowed between hit clusters). Indels shift the diagonal, so later gapped extension/chaining handles that.

- ★ Mention hash tables, suffix trees, suffix arrays — where do they appear in BLAST/BLAT?

  BLAST builds a lookup table (hash/finite-state automaton, "search tree" in the slides) of the query's neighbourhood words and scans the database. BLAT builds a hash table of non-overlapping $k$-mers of the genome (the database) kept in memory. Suffix trees/arrays and the BWT/FM-index are the basis of NGS read mappers (BWA, Bowtie) and allow exact queries of arbitrary length.

- ★ What is the time complexity — O()?

  Smith–Waterman against the database: $O(lL)$. BLAST: building the word list depends on $l$ and $T$ (≈ $l\cdot$#neighbours), scanning the database is $O(L)$ with $O(1)$ lookups, plus extension work proportional to the number of hits — so roughly linear in $L$ with a much smaller constant than DP. BLAT: each of the ~$l$ query words is an $O(1)$ hash lookup, so query time is roughly independent of genome size, but memory is $O(L)$. Suffix-array lookup $O(m\log N)$; FM-index $O(m)$.

- ★ When do you use BLAST vs BLAT?

  BLAT: DNA (or very similar protein) searches against a genome when the match is expected to be near-identical (≥ 95%) — mapping mRNA/ESTs to the genome, locating a sequence, many queries against the same genome; it is fast because the genome is pre-indexed but insensitive to divergent homologs. BLAST: general similarity searches, especially proteins and distant homologs, because neighbourhood words with BLOSUM62 find similar-but-not-identical seeds.

- What is a neighbourhood word and why is it needed?

  A word that scores at least $T$ against a query word under the substitution matrix (e.g. PEG for PQG, score 15). Exact matching of the query words only would miss related proteins whose words differ by conservative substitutions; neighbourhood words let us keep fast exact lookup but capture biological similarity.

- What does the threshold T control?

  Lower $T$ → more neighbourhood words → more hits → more sensitive but slower. Higher $T$ → faster, misses weak similarities. Similarly word length $k$.

- How is a hit extended? When does extension stop?

  Ungapped extension in both directions adding substitution scores; it continues through negative columns as long as the running score has not fallen more than $X$ below the best score so far (X-drop); the HSP is the best-scoring segment. Gapped extension (banded DP) then allows indels.

- Why is protein search more sensitive than DNA search? (Exercise.)

  The genetic code is degenerate: synonymous changes alter DNA but not protein, so proteins diverge more slowly; DNA scoring has only match/mismatch while protein matrices reward conservative substitutions. Also amino acids (20 letters) carry more information per position. In the exercise blastn didn't find *E. coli* Pol I from Taq, tblastn did.

- Which BLAST program for a DNA query against a protein database? Protein query vs genome?

  blastx (translate the query in 6 frames). tblastn (translate the database in 6 frames: 3 frames × 2 strands).

- Derive the probability of missing a true hit with word length k.

  With identity $q$ per position, a given word is identical with probability $q^k$; missing it, $1-q^k$; with $w$ (approximately independent) words in the true match, missing all $(1-q^k)^w$. Requiring two hits: also miss when exactly one matches, $wq^k(1-q^k)^{w-1}$. (Overlapping words are actually dependent, so this is approximate.)

- Expected number of random hits?

  Probability a random word matches $M^{-k}$; database contributes $L/k$ non-overlapping words (BLAT) and the query $l-k+1\approx l$ overlapping words → $\approx\frac Lk\,l\,M^{-k}$. For DNA, $k=11$: $4^{-11}\approx2.4\times10^{-7}$; against 3 Gb with a 1000-bp query → about $\frac{3\times10^9}{11}\times1000\times2.4\times10^{-7}\approx6.5\times10^4$ random hits — hence two-hit requirements.

- What does FASTA do differently?

  Exact $k$-tuple hits (1–2 protein, 4–6 DNA), find dense diagonals, rescore with a matrix, join regions, then Smith–Waterman in a band. BLAST added neighbourhood words and statistics.

- Why mask low-complexity regions?

  Repeats like poly-Q or AT-rich stretches match many unrelated sequences, generate huge numbers of seeds and inflate significance (they violate the random-background model).

- Can BLAST miss a homolog that Smith–Waterman finds?

  Yes — if no word scores above $T$ (no seed), or the extension stops early, the alignment is never computed; the final step cannot rescue regions discarded by the heuristic.
