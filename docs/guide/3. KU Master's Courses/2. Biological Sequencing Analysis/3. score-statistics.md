A database search tries so many alignments that even unrelated sequences sometimes score highly, so a score alone cannot say whether a hit is real. Score statistics tell how surprising a score is under a random model. The page first shows how bit scores and E-values are read and used for decisions, then how database size and the cutoff change the answer, and brings in the formulas only to support that interpretation.

::: tip Opening

Suppose I search a protein of unknown function against a large database and get a list of hits, each with a bit score and an E-value. The practical question is which hits are likely real homologues and which could have appeared by chance. I will first show how these numbers are read and used for decisions, then how database size and the cutoff change the answer, and bring in the formulas only to support that interpretation.

:::

## Which Hits Can I Trust {#why-a-high-score-needs-statistics}

The **raw score** depends on the scoring parameters; the **bit score** normalises it; the **E-value** counts expected chance hits.

::: table align="center" title="Reading E-values (rules of thumb)"

| E-value | Reading | Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\ll 10^{-5}$ | Chance explanation very unlikely | Candidate homologue; check coverage |
| $10^{-5}$ to about 0.01 | Probably real, weaker | Inspect alignment and domains |
| 0.01 to 1 | Twilight zone; contains some true homologues | Seek support (profile search) |
| $> 1$ | Expected by chance in this search | Do not interpret alone |

:::

These thresholds are conventions, not laws. ==The E-value is the expected number of chance hits at least this good in this search==: $E = 10$ means about ten such hits from unrelated sequences.

## The Same Alignment in a Larger Database {#e-value-and-search-space}

**Worked example.** A hit scores $S' = 30$ bits. With search space $mn = 10^6$, $E = 10^6/2^{30} \approx 9.31 \times 10^{-4}$: convincing. Search the same query against a 100-fold larger database and the same alignment has $E \approx 0.0931$ ($P \approx 0.0889$ of at least one chance hit): now borderline. Nothing about the alignment changed; the number of opportunities for chance did. To keep the original E-value it would need $\log_2 100 \approx 6.64$ more bits.

::: table align="center" title="How the E-value responds"

| Change, other factors fixed | Effect on E-value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Raw score increases by $\Delta S$ | Multiply by $e^{-\lambda \Delta S}$ |
| Effective database length $n \to 10n$ | Multiply by 10 |
| Effective search space $mn \to 2mn$ | Multiply by 2 |

:::

Consequences:

- E-values are comparable only within one search; to compare hits across databases or studies, use bit scores.
- Searching a smaller, targeted database (one genome, one family) makes weak true hits easier to detect.
- A longer query also enlarges the search space $mn$, so the same score becomes less surprising.

## Choosing a Cutoff {#choosing-a-cutoff}

To require $E \le E_0$, solve $S' \ge \log_2(mn/E_0)$. For $mn = 10^6$ and $E_0 = 0.01$, the cutoff is about 26.58 bits.

With many queries the expected false hits add up: annotating $10^4$ proteins at $E \le 0.01$ each still expects about 100 chance hits in total, so a stricter per-query cutoff is needed.

## Bit Scores {#bit-scores}

$$
S' = \frac{\lambda S - \ln K}{\ln 2}, \qquad E = mn\,2^{-S'}.
$$

Bit scores express scores in standardised statistical units and do not themselves include database size.

- At fixed search space, one extra bit halves the E-value.
- Doubling the database size requires one extra bit to maintain the E-value.
- A tenfold increase requires $\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$ additional bits.

## Interpretation and Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- A stricter E-value cutoff reduces chance hits but may miss weak homologues.
- Low complexity and composition bias violate the background model and can give tiny E-values without homology; masking or composition correction helps.
- A short identical match may still have a relatively large E-value, because short alignments cannot reach high scores.
- Inspect length, coverage, conserved domains and biological context.
- Statistical significance supports investigation; it does not establish identical function.

## E-value and Probability {#e-value-and-probability}

$$
E(S) = Kmn\,e^{-\lambda S}
$$

$S$ is the raw score, $m$ and $n$ the effective query and database lengths, and $K$ and $\lambda$ parameters determined by the scoring system and background. A smaller $E$ means stronger evidence against chance similarity.

$$
P(S_{\max} \le s) \approx \exp\!\left[-Kmn\,e^{-\lambda s}\right], \qquad P(S_{\max} \ge S) \approx 1 - e^{-E(S)}.
$$

> **Say:** Let $H$ count chance hits above the threshold. If $H$ is approximately Poisson with mean $E$, the chance of no hit is $e^{-E}$, so the chance of at least one is $1 - e^{-E}$.

$E$ is an expected count and may exceed 1; a probability cannot. For $E \ll 1$, $1 - e^{-E} \approx E$: $E = 0.01$ gives $P \approx 0.00995$, whereas $E = 2$ gives $P \approx 0.865$. ==Neither is a posterior probability of homology.==

## Why the Null Is an Extreme-Value Distribution {#extreme-values}

> **Say:** We report the best local score, so the relevant null distribution is a distribution of maxima, not of typical scores. Under suitable assumptions it is approximately Gumbel, not normal.

The Gumbel tail falls off exponentially, which is where $e^{-\lambda S}$ comes from. The background mean substitution score must be negative, with some positive scores, so chance alignments do not improve indefinitely by extension. Gapped searches use parameters calibrated by simulation.

## Where Scores Come From: Log-odds {#probabilistic-meaning-of-a-score}

For a fixed ungapped alignment, compare a related-sequence model $M$ with an independent background model $R$:

$$
\frac{P(X,Y \mid M)}{P(X,Y \mid R)} = \prod_i \frac{p_{x_i y_i}}{q_{x_i} q_{y_i}}, \qquad S = \sum_i \log \frac{p_{x_i y_i}}{q_{x_i} q_{y_i}}.
$$

$p_{ab}$ is an aligned-pair probability under the related model; $q_a$ and $q_b$ are background residue frequencies. Positive scores favour related pairing over chance. PAM and BLOSUM provide the substitution scores; gapped alignments also subtract gap penalties. Raw scores depend on the matrix and gap costs and cannot be compared directly across scoring schemes.

::: tip Whiteboard route

Hit list and question → E-value as expected chance hits → same hit in a larger database → cutoff and bit-score rules → pitfalls → support: $E = Kmn\,e^{-\lambda S}$, $P = 1 - e^{-E}$, $S'$ and $E = mn\,2^{-S'}$.

Closing: alignment scores measure match quality; score statistics assess how surprising it is under a random model. E-values include the search-space size; bit scores normalise the scoring system.

:::

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

**The practical question.** Suppose I search a protein of unknown function against a large database and BLAST returns a list of hits, each with a bit score and an E-value. The question I have to answer is practical: which of these hits can I trust as real homologues, and which could simply be the best of millions of chance alignments? An alignment score alone cannot answer this, because a database search tries so many alignments that even unrelated sequences will sometimes score highly. Score statistics tell me how surprising a score is under a random model.

**What the E-value means.** The E-value of a hit with score $S$ is the expected number of alignments with a score at least $S$ that would occur by chance in a search of this size. It is a count, not a probability. An E-value of 10 means I expect about ten hits this good from unrelated sequences, so such a hit means nothing by itself. An E-value of $10^{-20}$ means chance is not a reasonable explanation. As a rule of thumb, hits far below $10^{-5}$ are treated as candidate homologues, hits between about 0.01 and 1 are a twilight zone that needs further support, and hits above 1 should not be interpreted alone. These are conventions, not laws.

**The formula, as support.** For local alignments the expected number of chance hits is $E = Kmn\,e^{-\lambda S}$. Here $m$ and $n$ are the effective lengths of the query and the database, so $mn$ is the size of the search space, and $K$ and $\lambda$ are constants that depend on the scoring matrix and the background residue frequencies. The formula says two things that I use in practice. First, $E$ falls exponentially with the score: a somewhat better alignment is very much more significant. Second, $E$ grows linearly with the search space: the same alignment is ten times less significant in a database ten times larger.

**A scenario that shows why this matters.** Say a hit scores 30 bits. In a search space of $10^6$ its E-value is $10^6/2^{30}$, about $10^{-3}$: convincing. Now I run the same query against a database one hundred times larger. The alignment is exactly the same, but its E-value is now about 0.09: borderline. Nothing about the biology has changed; only the number of opportunities for a chance match has. Two consequences follow. E-values can only be compared within one search, and if I want to detect a weak but real relationship I should search a smaller, targeted database, such as one genome or one protein family, instead of everything.

**Bit scores.** Raw scores depend on the scoring matrix and gap costs, so a raw score of 80 under one matrix cannot be compared with 80 under another. The bit score removes this dependence: $S' = (\lambda S - \ln K)/\ln 2$, and then simply $E = mn\,2^{-S'}$. This gives easy rules: each additional bit halves the E-value; doubling the database costs one bit; a tenfold larger database costs about 3.3 bits. The bit score does not contain the database size, so it is the right number for comparing hits from different searches or different papers.

**Choosing a cutoff.** If I want at most $E_0$ expected false hits, I need a bit score of at least $\log_2(mn/E_0)$; for a search space of $10^6$ and $E_0 = 0.01$ that is about 26.6 bits. The cutoff is a trade-off: a strict cutoff gives few false positives but misses weak, distant homologues; a relaxed cutoff finds more of them but also more noise. If I run many queries, for example annotating ten thousand proteins, the expected false hits add up: at $E \le 0.01$ per query I still expect about a hundred chance hits in total, so I should be stricter.

**Where the formula comes from.** Two ideas. First, the score itself is a log-odds: it measures how much better the alignment is explained by a model of related sequences than by a model of independent random sequences. Second, the score that BLAST reports is the best local score, a maximum over a very large number of possible alignments. The maximum of many random variables does not follow a normal distribution; it follows an extreme-value distribution, the Gumbel distribution, whose tail falls off exponentially. That is where $e^{-\lambda S}$ comes from. For this to hold, the expected score of a random pair of residues must be negative; otherwise random alignments would just grow longer and longer.

**E-value versus P-value.** If the number of chance hits is approximately Poisson with mean $E$, the probability of seeing at least one is $P = 1 - e^{-E}$. For small $E$ the two are almost equal, for example $E = 0.01$ gives $P \approx 0.00995$. For large $E$ they differ: $E = 2$ gives $P \approx 0.86$. An E-value can be larger than one; a probability cannot. Neither of them is the probability that the two sequences are homologous.

**Pitfalls.** The statistics assume a random background. Low-complexity or compositionally biased regions violate it and can give tiny E-values without any homology, so I mask them or use composition correction. A short perfect match can still have a large E-value because short alignments cannot reach high scores. And statistical significance supports homology, not identical function: I still look at coverage, conserved domains and the biological context.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- What exactly is an E-value? Is it a probability?

  It is the expected number of chance alignments scoring at least as well as this hit in a search of this size. It is a count and can exceed one. The corresponding probability of at least one such chance hit is $1 - e^{-E}$, which is close to $E$ only when $E$ is small.

- ★ The same hit has $E = 10^{-4}$ in one database and $10^{-2}$ in another. Why does the E-value increase when the database grows?

  The second database is about a hundred times larger. $E$ is proportional to the search space $mn$, so with the same alignment and bit score the E-value scales with the database size. The alignment has not become worse; there are simply more chances for a random match.

- ★ Why does it increase with a longer query too?

  The search space $mn$ grows with the query length $m$. A local alignment score cannot get worse when the query gets longer, since the alignment can ignore the extra part, and the extra positions provide more chances for a high random score, so the maximum random score shifts upwards (roughly $\ln(mn)/\lambda$). A fixed score $S$ is therefore less surprising.

- ★ Should the E-value include the chance of missing a word?

  No. The E-value counts expected *chance* alignments with score at least $S$, derived from the score distribution of random sequences. The chance that the heuristic misses a true homologue (no seed) concerns sensitivity and false negatives and is not part of the E-value.

- Why do we need bit scores if we have raw scores?

  Raw scores depend on the matrix and gap penalties through $K$ and $\lambda$. The bit score absorbs these constants, so bit scores from different scoring systems and different searches can be compared, and the E-value is simply $mn\,2^{-S'}$.

- Why is the distribution of scores an extreme-value distribution and not a normal one?

  Because the reported score is the maximum over a huge number of possible local alignments, not a sum or an average. Maxima of many random variables follow an extreme-value distribution with an exponentially decaying right tail.

- How would you choose an E-value cutoff for annotating a whole genome?

  I would think about the total number of expected false hits: the number of queries times the cutoff. With thousands of queries I would use a strict cutoff such as $10^{-5}$ or lower, and add requirements on coverage, because a hit to a single shared domain does not justify transferring the function of the whole protein.

- A hit has a very small E-value but the alignment is a stretch of repeated serines. What do you think?

  That it is probably an artefact of low complexity. The statistical model assumes typical residue composition; a biased region matches many unrelated sequences. I would turn on masking or composition-based statistics and look for a hit outside that region.

- Does an E-value of $10^{-30}$ mean the two proteins have the same function?

  It means the similarity is not due to chance, so the proteins are almost certainly homologous. Function may still differ, for example between paralogues or when only one domain is shared, so I check coverage, domain architecture and key functional residues.

:::
