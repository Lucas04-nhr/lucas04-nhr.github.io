A database search makes millions of comparisons, so unrelated sequences will sometimes score highly by chance. This topic is about quantifying that: why maxima follow an extreme-value distribution, what the E-value counts, and how it responds to score, query length and database size.

## Why a High Score Needs Statistics {#why-a-high-score-needs-statistics}

A database search tests many possible matches, so even unrelated sequences can produce high scores. The raw score depends on the scoring parameters, the bit score normalises it, and the E-value counts expected chance hits.

## Probabilistic Meaning of a Score {#probabilistic-meaning-of-a-score}

$$
\frac{P(X,Y \mid M)}{P(X,Y \mid R)} = \prod_i \frac{p_{x_i y_i}}{q_{x_i} q_{y_i}}, \qquad
S = \sum_i \log \frac{p_{x_i y_i}}{q_{x_i} q_{y_i}}
$$

Positive scores favour related pairing over chance. Gapped alignments also subtract gap penalties. Raw scores depend on the matrix and gap costs, so they cannot be compared directly across scoring schemes.

## Extreme Values {#extreme-values}

What is reported is the best local score, so the relevant null distribution is that of a maximum, not of a typical score. Under suitable assumptions it is approximately a Gumbel (extreme-value) distribution rather than normal. This requires the expected substitution score under the background to be negative while some scores are positive, so random alignments do not keep improving with length. Gapped searches use parameters calibrated by simulation.

The lecture analogy: heights of all people are roughly normal, but the height of the tallest person in each group is shifted and has a long right tail; likewise average rainfall versus the 100-year flood. For ungapped alignment (Karlin–Altschul theory), $\lambda$ is the unique positive solution of

$$
\sum_{a,b} q_a q_b\, e^{\lambda s(a,b)} = 1,
$$

and $K$ corrects for the search space. Effective lengths correct for edge effects, since an alignment cannot start near the very end of a sequence.

## E-value and Search Space {#e-value-and-search-space}

$$
E(S) = Kmn\,e^{-\lambda S}
$$

$m, n$ are effective query and database lengths; $K$ and $\lambda$ depend on the scoring system and background.

$$
P(S_{\max} \le x) \approx \exp\!\left[-Kmn\,e^{-\lambda x}\right], \qquad
P(S_{\max} \ge S) \approx 1 - e^{-E(S)}
$$

The probability follows from a Poisson argument: if the number of chance hits above the threshold is approximately Poisson with mean $E$, the probability of none is $e^{-E}$. $E$ is a count and may exceed 1; a probability cannot. For $E \ll 1$, $1 - e^{-E} \approx E$. Neither is a posterior probability of homology.

::: table align="center" title="How the E-value responds"

| Change (other factors fixed) | Effect on E-value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Raw score increases by $\Delta S$ | Multiply by $e^{-\lambda \Delta S}$ |
| Database length $n \to 10n$ | Multiply by 10 |
| Search space $mn \to 2mn$ | Multiply by 2 |

:::

The same alignment receives a larger E-value in a larger database. For illustration, $E = 0.01$ gives $P \approx 0.00995$, whereas $E = 2$ gives $P \approx 0.865$.

Why does a longer query increase $E$? The search space $mn$ grows, and the best local score of a longer random query can never be worse than that of any of its substrings, because the extra residues can simply be ignored. The maximum random score therefore grows roughly like $\ln(mn)/\lambda$, and a fixed score becomes less surprising. The slides also give the approximation $E \approx pD$, where $p$ is the per-sequence P-value and $D$ the number of database sequences, which shows the same linear dependence on database size. The exercise used $E \le 10^{-3}$ as cutoff. With $\lambda \approx 0.32$ (BLOSUM62, ungapped), a raw score increase of 10 reduces $E$ about 25-fold.

## Bit Scores {#bit-scores}

$$
S' = \frac{\lambda S - \ln K}{\ln 2}, \qquad E = mn\,2^{-S'}
$$

Bit scores are expressed in standardised units and do not themselves include database size. At fixed search space, one extra bit halves the E-value; doubling the database requires one extra bit to keep the same E-value; a tenfold increase requires $\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$ bits.

**Worked example.** Let $S' = 30$ bits and $mn = 10^6$. Then $E = 10^6 / 2^{30} \approx 9.31 \times 10^{-4}$ and $P \approx 0.000931$. In a 100-fold larger database, $E \approx 0.0931$ and $P \approx 0.0889$; keeping the original E-value needs $\log_2 100 \approx 6.64$ more bits. To require $E \le E_0$, solve $S' \ge \log_2(mn/E_0)$: for $mn = 10^6$ and $E_0 = 0.01$, the cutoff is about 26.58 bits.

## Pitfalls {#pitfalls}

- A stricter cutoff reduces chance hits but may miss weak homologues.
- Low complexity and composition bias violate the background model; masking or composition correction helps.
- A short identical match can still have a relatively large E-value.
- Statistical significance supports further investigation; it does not establish identical function.

- The E-value does **not** include the chance that the heuristic missed a seed. It counts chance (false-positive) hits; missed true homologues are a sensitivity (false-negative) issue of seeding.
- The E-value is not the probability that two sequences are unrelated, nor a posterior probability of homology. Check alignment length, coverage, identity and domain architecture as well.

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

My topic is score statistics and E-values in database searches. When BLAST compares a query to a database, it effectively tests millions of possible local alignments. Even if the query is unrelated to everything in the database, some alignments will get a fairly high score just by chance. So the raw score alone does not tell us whether a hit is meaningful. We need to know how surprising a score is, given the scoring system and the size of the search. That is what the E-value provides.

First, where do scores come from? Substitution scores are log-odds scores: the log of the probability of seeing residues *a* and *b* aligned in related sequences, divided by the probability of seeing them together by chance, $q_aq_b$.

$$
s(a,b)=\log\frac{p_{ab}}{q_aq_b},\qquad S=\sum s\ -\ \text{gap penalties}
$$

So the alignment score compares a "related" model with a "random" model. The raw score depends on the matrix and gap penalties, so raw scores are not comparable between searches with different scoring systems. For the statistical theory — Karlin and Altschul — two conditions must hold: the expected score of a random pair must be negative, and at least one positive score must be possible. Otherwise local alignments would simply grow with length.

Second, which distribution do random scores follow? The key point is that a local alignment search reports the *best* score — the maximum over a huge number of possible alignments. Maxima do not follow a normal distribution. The lecture's analogy: the heights of all people are roughly normal, but if we take the tallest person in each group, that distribution is shifted and has a long right tail — it is unlikely that the tallest person is shorter than average, and sometimes the tallest is very tall. Similarly with yearly rainfall versus the worst flood in a hundred years. The distribution of maximal alignment scores is an extreme value, or Gumbel, distribution, whose right tail decays exponentially.

From this tail we get the E-value formula:

$$
E(S)=K\,m\,n\,e^{-\lambda S}
$$

Here $m$ is the effective length of the query, $n$ the effective total length of the database, so $mn$ is the size of the search space, and $K$ and $\lambda$ are constants that depend on the scoring matrix and background frequencies — for gapped alignments they are estimated by simulation. The E-value is the expected number of alignments with score at least $S$ that we would find by chance in a search of this size. It is a count, not a probability, and it can be larger than one. An E-value of 1 means: in a database of this size, one hit this good is expected by chance. The smaller the E-value, the more significant; a common cutoff is $10^{-3}$, as in the exercise.

To get a probability: if the number of chance hits is approximately Poisson with mean $E$, the probability of no chance hit is $e^{-E}$, so the probability of at least one chance hit with score at least $S$ is

$$
P=1-e^{-E}\approx E\quad\text{for small }E
$$

For small $E$ the two are almost equal: $E=0.01$ gives $P\approx0.00995$; but $E=2$ gives $P\approx0.86$. As the score increases, $e^{-\lambda S}$ goes to zero, so $E$ and $P$ go to zero — very high scores are very unlikely by chance.

Now the important behaviour. Holding everything else fixed: if the database becomes ten times larger, the E-value becomes ten times larger, because there are ten times more opportunities for a chance match. The same alignment can be significant in a small database and not significant in a huge one. If the query becomes longer, the search space also grows, so again $E$ increases. One way to see why is that a local alignment can never get worse when the query gets longer — the alignment can simply ignore the extra residues — so the best random score grows, roughly like the logarithm of $mn$ divided by $\lambda$, and a given score becomes less surprising. And if the score increases by $\Delta S$, the E-value is multiplied by $e^{-\lambda\Delta S}$ — an exponential decrease.

Because raw scores depend on the scoring system, BLAST also reports a bit score, which normalises out $K$ and $\lambda$:

$$
S'=\frac{\lambda S-\ln K}{\ln 2},\qquad E=mn\,2^{-S'}
$$

Bit scores can be compared between searches. Each extra bit halves the E-value, and doubling the database requires one more bit to keep the same E-value. For example, a 30-bit hit with a search space of $10^6$ has an E-value of $10^6/2^{30}$, about $10^{-3}$. In a database a hundred times larger the same alignment has an E-value around $0.09$ — no longer convincing. To keep $E\le0.01$ in a search space of $10^6$ we need about 26.6 bits.

Finally, interpretation and pitfalls. The E-value is not the probability that two sequences are unrelated, and it is not a probability of homology. It only controls false positives: it does not tell us about true homologs that the heuristic missed because no seed was found — that is a sensitivity issue. The statistics assume a random background model; low-complexity or compositionally biased sequences violate that and give falsely small E-values, so they are masked or composition-corrected. A short perfect match can still have a large E-value because the maximum possible score is small. And statistical significance supports homology, but does not prove identical function — we should also look at coverage, alignment length, domain architecture, as we did when the Taq polymerase hits in *E. coli* covered only part of the query. *The same ideas — log-odds scores, a background model and an expected count under chance — come back in motif discovery with Poisson word counts.*

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: collapse

- ★ Should the E-value include the chance of missing a word?

  No. The E-value is the expected number of *chance* (false-positive) alignments with score ≥ S in the search space; it is derived for the score distribution of random sequences. The chance that the heuristic misses a true homolog (no seed) concerns sensitivity/false negatives and is not part of the E-value. (BLAST's parameters are calibrated so that seeding rarely affects high-scoring hits.)

- ★ Why does the E-value increase when the database grows?

  $E=Kmne^{-\lambda S}$ is proportional to $n$: more database sequence = more independent opportunities for an unrelated sequence to reach score $S$ by chance. The same alignment therefore becomes less significant in a larger database. 10× database → 10× E.

- ★ Why does it increase with a longer query too?

  The search space $mn$ grows with the query length $m$. A local alignment score cannot get worse when the query gets longer — the alignment can simply ignore the extra part — and the extra positions provide additional chances for a high random score, so the maximum random score shifts upwards (≈ $\ln(mn)/\lambda$). Therefore a fixed score $S$ is less surprising.

- Why an extreme value distribution and not a normal distribution?

  Because the reported score is the maximum over many local alignments. Maxima of many i.i.d. variables (with exponential-type tails) follow the Gumbel distribution, which is skewed with a long right tail; using a normal distribution would underestimate the probability of high scores. Tallest person per group / 100-year flood analogies.

- What are K and λ?

  Parameters of the extreme value distribution: $\lambda$ scales the score (it is the unique positive solution of $\sum_{a,b}q_aq_be^{\lambda s(a,b)}=1$ for ungapped alignment), $K$ is a search-space correction. They depend on the matrix and background composition; for gapped alignments they are estimated by simulation of random sequences.

- E-value vs P-value? Can E be larger than 1?

  E = expected number of chance hits (a count, any non-negative number). P = probability of at least one chance hit $=1-e^{-E}$ (Poisson). For $E<0.01$, $P\approx E$. E = 2 → P = 0.86. E can exceed 1, P cannot.

- Interpret E = 1, E = $10^{-50}$.

  E = 1: we'd expect one such hit by chance in this search — not significant. E = $10^{-50}$: essentially impossible by chance — strong evidence of homology (but not of identical function).

- What is a bit score and why use it?

  $S'=(\lambda S-\ln K)/\ln2$ normalises the raw score for the scoring system, so bit scores from different matrices are comparable, and $E=mn2^{-S'}$. +1 bit halves E; doubling the database requires +1 bit for the same E.

- How does E change if the score increases by 10 raw units?

  Multiplied by $e^{-10\lambda}$ — exponential decrease (with $\lambda\approx0.32$ for BLOSUM62 ungapped, ~25-fold).

- Why might a short perfect match have a large E-value?

  A short alignment has a low maximum score, and short exact matches are common by chance in a large database.

- What can make E-values misleading?

  Low-complexity / compositional bias (violates background), repeats, very short sequences (edge effects; effective lengths correct for this), multiple testing across many queries, non-independence of database sequences, assuming significance implies same function.

- E ≈ pD — what does that mean?

  For a per-sequence probability $p$ of a chance score ≥ S, searching $D$ independent database sequences gives expected count $pD$ — the same linear dependence on database size.

- What is the relationship between alignment score and log-odds?

  The raw score is (proportional to) the log-likelihood ratio of the match model vs the random model; so a high score means the alignment is much more likely if the sequences are related. The statistics then tell us how often such a ratio arises from unrelated sequences in this search.

:::
