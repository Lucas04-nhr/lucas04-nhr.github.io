This topic has two halves: the sequencing technologies and data formats, and the computational core, which is how the Burrows–Wheeler transform and FM-index make it possible to map millions of reads to a reference. The revision session asked for a complete BWT walkthrough, including decoding.

## Sequencing Technologies {#sequencing-technologies}

- **Sanger (first generation):** dideoxy chain termination and capillary electrophoresis; one template per reaction; reads of about 700–1000 bp with very high accuracy (~99.99%); low throughput and high cost per base (Human Genome Project).
- **Second generation (short reads, massively parallel):** 454 (pyrosequencing, discontinued), SOLiD (ligation, discontinued), **Illumina** (sequencing by synthesis with reversible fluorescent terminators on clonal clusters from bridge amplification), MGI/DNBSEQ, Ultima.
- **Third generation (single molecule, long reads):** **Oxford Nanopore** (a single strand ratchets through a protein pore and the ionic current is decoded; kb to > 1 Mb reads, real time, portable MinION, native DNA/RNA and base modifications, no amplification) and **PacBio** (SMRT; HiFi circular consensus reads of ~15–20 kb at > 99.9% accuracy).

::: table align="center" title="Illumina versus Nanopore"

| | Illumina (2nd gen) | Nanopore (3rd gen) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Principle | Sequencing by synthesis on clusters | Current through a pore, single molecule |
| Read length | 50–300 bp | kb to Mb |
| Errors | Low, mostly substitutions; quality drops towards read ends | Higher, mainly indels in homopolymers |
| Throughput / cost per base | Very high / low | Lower / higher (cheap device) |
| Strengths | SNPs, counting (RNA-seq), high depth, aDNA | De novo assembly, repeats, structural variants, phasing, full-length isoforms, methylation, field work |
| Weaknesses | Repeats, SVs, phasing, PCR/GC bias | Accuracy, homopolymers |

:::

Short reads suit accurate SNP and genotype calling, population data and quantification, and molecules that are short anyway (ancient DNA of ~30–70 bp gains nothing from long reads). Long reads suit assembly, repeats, structural variants, phasing, full-length transcripts, direct RNA/methylation and real-time work. Hybrid approaches combine both.

The Illumina process in brief: fragment DNA → end repair and A-tailing → ligate adapters with indexes → bridge amplification into clusters on the flow cell → cycles of fluorescent reversible terminators with imaging → base calls with Phred qualities → demultiplex → FASTQ. To sequence RNA on Illumina, it must first be reverse-transcribed into cDNA; Nanopore can read native RNA directly.

## Fragments, Reads and Libraries {#fragments-reads-libraries}

A **fragment** (insert) is the physical DNA molecule between the adapters; a **read** is the sequence the instrument reports from one end (single-end) or both ends (paired-end). A 500-bp fragment with 2×150 reads leaves 200 bp unsequenced in the middle; if the fragment is shorter than the read length, the read runs into the adapter, which must be trimmed, and the paired reads overlap and can be merged. Insert size is inferred from paired-end mapping.

A sequencing **library** is the collection of adapter-ligated fragments. Its **complexity** is the number of distinct original molecules and its **clonality** the proportion of duplicates. Sequencing deeper into a low-complexity library re-reads the same molecules (PCR duplicates): depth rises without new information.

## Data, Quality and Workflow {#data-quality-and-workflow}

Next-generation sequencing produces many reads from sampled DNA fragments. Illumina uses sequencing by synthesis on an adapter-prepared library; paired-end reads cover both fragment ends. A typical pipeline:

```text
sample → library → sequencing → FASTQ → QC / adapter trimming
       → reference mapping → SAM/BAM → filtering, variant and genotype inference → VCF
```

FASTA stores identifiers and sequences; FASTQ stores four lines per read (ID, sequence, `+`, per-base quality string); SAM/BAM store alignment records (reference, position, flag, MAPQ, CIGAR, sequence, qualities); VCF stores variants and genotypes.

Base quality and mapping quality both use the Phred scale but describe different uncertainties:

$$
Q = -10 \log_{10} P(\text{error}), \qquad P(\text{error}) = 10^{-Q/10}
$$

Q20 means a 1% error probability and Q30 means 0.1%; FASTQ usually encodes $Q$ as ASCII $Q + 33$. A read in a repeat can have excellent base qualities and still a low MAPQ, because its placement is uncertain.

Depth counts reads covering a position; breadth is the fraction of positions covered. Under uniform independent sampling, $D \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and $P(D \ge 1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda}$, so a given mean depth does not guarantee every site is covered. Duplicates add reads without independent evidence. Ancient DNA brings short fragments, low endogenous content, contamination and low complexity; deamination (C→U) shows up as terminal C→T or G→A, which causes errors but also helps authenticate genuinely ancient molecules.

A few record-reading details: SAM `POS` is 1-based and `FLAG` is a bit mask. The CIGAR `5M1I4M2D3M` consumes 13 read bases and 14 reference bases; `M` covers matches and mismatches, `I`/`D` are relative to the reference, and `S` clips read bases. Genotypes are inferred with $P(G \mid D) \propto P(D \mid G) P(G)$; in a VCF, `0/1` means REF and the first ALT, unphased.

## Ancient DNA {#ancient-dna}

Ancient DNA is **short** (the backbone breaks post mortem), **damaged** (cytosine deamination C→U, read as T, gives excess C→T at 5′ ends and G→A at 3′ ends in the first 1–25 bp), present at a **low endogenous fraction** (mostly environmental bacteria), and at risk of **contamination** by modern human DNA. Damage is a nuisance, but also an authenticity signal. Petrous bone and teeth are the best samples, and cold, dry, stable environments preserve DNA best. On the slides, 20 M reads of 50 bp give about 0.003× depth at 1% endogenous content, 0.05× at 15% and 0.8× at 100%.

The exercise workflow:

```text
raw FASTQ → QC, adapter trimming, merging of overlapping pairs (fastp)
          → map with BWA aln (short reads; samse/sampe)
          → sort, samtools fixmate + markdup, remove duplicates
          → filter (mapped, MAPQ, length from CIGAR)
          → mapDamage (C→T / G→A profile)
          → genotype likelihoods → population genetics
```

Trimming and merging are only appropriate for short aDNA fragments; long modern fragments that cannot be collapsed would be lost.

With low depth, a heterozygote need not show a 50:50 allele ratio: reads sample the two chromosomes with replacement, so at depth 4 the probability of seeing only one allele is $2 \cdot 0.5^4 = 12.5\%$. This is why genotype likelihoods are preferred over hard calls.

## Why Index the Reference {#why-index-the-reference}

Millions of reads must be mapped to a large genome while tolerating errors and real differences. Full DP for every read is too expensive, so the reference is preprocessed once. With reference length $m$ (including `$`) and query length $n$:

::: table align="center" title="Reference indexes compared"

| Index | Representation | Exact search |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Suffix tree | Compressed tree of suffixes | $O(n)$ |
| Suffix array | Sorted suffix starts | $O(n \log m)$ |
| FM-index | Compressed BWT plus rank support | $O(n)$, assuming constant-time rank |

:::

The FM-index search counts occurrences; locating all coordinates is extra. Its practical memory footprint is far smaller than an explicit suffix tree.

## Building the BWT {#building-the-bwt}

Append `$`, sort all cyclic rotations, and take the last column $L$; the first column is $F$. For BANANA\$:

$$
SA = (7,6,4,2,1,5,3), \qquad F = \texttt{\$AAABNN}, \qquad L = \texttt{ANNB\$AA}
$$

Row 3, for instance, is `ANA$BAN`: its suffix starts at 4 and the preceding character is N. Each $L[i]$ is the character immediately before the corresponding suffix. In practice the BWT is derived from the suffix array without materialising the rotation matrix. The transform is reversible and groups similar contexts, which makes compression easier, but it does not shorten the string by itself.

## LF Mapping {#lf-mapping}

The key property is occurrence order: the $k$-th A in $L$ corresponds to the $k$-th A in $F$. With 1-based inclusive indices:

$$
C[c] = \#\{\text{characters} < c\}, \qquad \mathrm{Occ}(c, i) = \#\{c \text{ in } L[1..i]\}, \qquad
LF(i) = C[L[i]] + \mathrm{Occ}(L[i], i)
$$

$LF$ moves to the row whose suffix starts one position earlier in the reference, so repeated LF steps reconstruct the text backwards from `$`. An FM-index stores the BWT, $C$, rank support for $\mathrm{Occ}$, and usually a sampled suffix array for locating hits, trading memory against locating time.

## Inverting the BWT {#inverting-the-bwt}

::: table align="center" title="Burrows–Wheeler matrix of BANANA$"

| Row | Sorted rotation | F | L | SA |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | `$BANANA` | \$ | A | 7 |
| 2 | `A$BANAN` | A | N | 6 |
| 3 | `ANA$BAN` | A | N | 4 |
| 4 | `ANANA$B` | A | B | 2 |
| 5 | `BANANA$` | B | \$ | 1 |
| 6 | `NA$BANA` | N | A | 5 |
| 7 | `NANA$BA` | N | A | 3 |

:::

With $C$: \$ = 0, A = 1, B = 4, N = 5, start at row 1 (the row beginning with `$`); $L[1] = \text{A}$ is the last real letter of the text.

::: table align="center" title="LF walk that decodes BANANA"

| Step | Row $i$ | $L[i]$ | $LF(i)$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 1 | A | $1 + 1 = 2$ |
| 2 | 2 | N | $5 + 1 = 6$ |
| 3 | 6 | A | $1 + 2 = 3$ |
| 4 | 3 | N | $5 + 2 = 7$ |
| 5 | 7 | A | $1 + 3 = 4$ |
| 6 | 4 | B | $4 + 1 = 5$ (row with L = \$, stop) |

:::

The collected letters A, N, A, N, A, B read backwards give BANANA. The slides also show a conceptually simple but slow alternative: repeatedly prepend $L$ to the sorted columns and re-sort to rebuild the whole matrix.

Why the BWT compresses: rows are sorted by what follows, so characters preceding the same context are adjacent (both suffixes starting with ANA are preceded by N, giving the run NN). Genomic repeats produce long runs that compress well with run-length or move-to-front coding. The BWT itself is a lossless permutation of the same length.

## Backward Search {#backward-search}

Process the query right to left, keeping the interval $[l, r]$ of sorted rows whose suffixes begin with the part matched so far. Start with $[1, m]$. To prepend $c$:

$$
l' = C[c] + \mathrm{Occ}(c, l-1) + 1, \qquad r' = C[c] + \mathrm{Occ}(c, r)
$$

If $l' > r'$ there is no exact match. At the end, the count is $r - l + 1$, and coordinates come from $SA[l..r]$ or from LF walks to sampled positions.

**Worked example.** Reference `AGACCCATGACC$`, BWT `CGG$CCCACATAA`:

::: table align="center" title="C table for AGACCCATGACC$"

| $c$ | `$` | A | C | G | T |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $C[c]$ | 0 | 1 | 5 | 10 | 12 |

:::

Searching GACC:

::: table align="center" title="Backward search for GACC"

| Character added | Pattern matched | Interval |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| C | C | [6, 10] |
| C | CC | [8, 10] |
| A | ACC | [2, 3] |
| G | GACC | [11, 12] |

:::

For example, extending C to CC gives $l' = 5 + \mathrm{Occ}(C, 5) + 1 = 5 + 2 + 1 = 8$ and $r' = 5 + \mathrm{Occ}(C, 10) = 10$. The final count is 2 and the reference starts are 2 and 9; the interval [11, 12] contains row numbers, not genomic coordinates. As an LF check, $L[6] = \text{C}$ and $LF(6) = 5 + 3 = 8$.

**BANANA example.** For the query ANA: A gives $[C[\text{A}]+1, C[\text{B}]] = [2,4]$; prepending N gives $l = 5 + \mathrm{Occ}(N,1) + 1 = 6$ and $r = 5 + \mathrm{Occ}(N,4) = 7$, i.e. rows 6–7 (`NA$`, `NANA$`); prepending A gives $l = 1 + \mathrm{Occ}(A,5) + 1 = 3$ and $r = 1 + \mathrm{Occ}(A,7) = 4$. The interval [3, 4] has size 2, and $SA[3..4]$ gives positions 4 and 2.

::: table align="center" title="Occ counts for BANANA$"

| $i$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| L | A | N | N | B | \$ | A | A |
| Occ(A, i) | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Occ(N, i) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Occ(B, i) | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

:::

The FM-index needs about 0.5 bytes per base (≈ 1.5 GB for the human genome, against ≈ 12 GB for a suffix array and ≈ 45 GB for a suffix tree).

## From Exact Search to Read Alignment {#from-exact-search-to-read-alignment}

Exact backward search cannot handle mismatches or indels on its own, so mappers use seed-and-extend: find seeds with the index, locate and group compatible hits, extend candidates with mismatches and gaps, compare alternatives, and assign MAPQ. BWA-aln seeds from the first bases of the read (configurable length) before extending; BWA-MEM finds maximal exact matches, chains compatible hits and extends them with local alignment. Short reads in repeats create alternative placements, while longer reads and paired-end constraints improve specificity.

BWA aln uses a backtracking search in the BWT that allows a few differences, bounded by a lower bound $D(i)$ on the remaining differences; it suits reads shorter than about 70 bp and is preferred for aDNA. BWA-MEM uses super-maximal exact matches (SMEMs) as seeds and banded Smith–Waterman extension, and also handles split reads. Common BWT aligners are extremely fast but not base-quality aware.

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Keyword Card {#keyword-card}

- **Sanger** (1st, ~1 kb, accurate, low throughput) → **Illumina** (SBS, 50–300 bp, PE, substitutions) → **Nanopore/PacBio** (single molecule, long, indels/homopolymers, real time, methylation)
- Short: SNPs, counts, depth, aDNA; Long: assembly, repeats, SV, phasing, isoforms
- **Fragment** (insert between adapters) vs **read** (sequenced end[s]); short insert → adapter read-through, merge
- **Workflow**: library → FASTQ → trim → map → BAM → dedup → genotype → VCF
- **FASTQ** 4 lines; Phred $Q=-10\log_{10}\epsilon$ (Q20 1%, Q30 0.1%); **SAM**: POS, CIGAR, **MAPQ ≠ base Q**
- Depth Poisson; genotype likelihoods at low depth
- **Why index**: millions of reads × 3 Gb; build once
- **BWT**: rotations, sort, last column; BANANA$ → **ANNB$AA**; = char before each sorted suffix
- **Compression**: same context → runs (NN); lossless, same length
- **LF**: k-th c in L = k-th c in F; $LF(i)=C[L[i]]+Occ(L[i],i)$; decode 1→2→6→3→7→4: BANANA
- **FM-index** = BWT + C + Occ + sampled SA; 0.5 B/base (1.5 GB vs 12 vs 45)
- **Backward search** ANA: [2,4] → [6,7] → [3,4] → pos 4, 2; GACC → [11,12] → 2, 9; $O(m)$
- **BWA aln** (32-bp seed, backtracking, short/aDNA) vs **BWA mem** (SMEMs, chain, SW)
- **Limits**: reference bias, repeats → MAPQ, duplicates, aDNA C→T

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

My topic is next-generation sequencing, and in particular how the Burrows–Wheeler transform makes it possible to map millions of reads to a reference genome.

Before NGS we had Sanger sequencing: chain termination with dideoxy nucleotides, one template per reaction, reads of up to about a thousand bases with very high accuracy, but low throughput — the Human Genome Project took years. Second-generation sequencing, dominated by Illumina, sequences millions of clusters in parallel by synthesis: in each cycle a fluorescently labelled reversible terminator is incorporated and imaged. Reads are short, 50 to 300 bases, often paired-end, with low error rates, mostly substitutions. Third-generation technologies like Oxford Nanopore read single long molecules — a DNA strand passes through a protein pore and the change in current is decoded — giving reads of many kilobases up to megabases, in real time, with higher error rates, especially indels in homopolymers. Short reads are best for accurate SNP calling and counting at high depth; long reads for assembly, repeats, structural variants and phasing.

It is important to distinguish a fragment from a read. The fragment, or insert, is the physical DNA molecule in the library, between the adapters. The read is what the machine reports — one end, or both ends with paired-end sequencing. If the fragment is shorter than the read length, the read runs into the adapter, which must be trimmed — this is typical for ancient DNA, which is short and damaged.

The standard workflow is: sample, library, sequencing, FASTQ, quality control and trimming, mapping to the reference, a BAM file, duplicate removal and filtering, and finally genotypes in a VCF file.

The raw output is FASTQ: four lines per read — identifier, sequence, plus, and a quality string. Each quality character encodes a Phred score, $Q=-10\log_{10}$ of the error probability, so Q20 is a 1 percent error and Q30 0.1 percent. After mapping we have SAM or BAM files with, per read, the reference name, position, the CIGAR string describing matches, insertions and deletions, and the mapping quality, MAPQ — the confidence in the position, which is different from base quality: a read in a repeat can have perfect bases but MAPQ zero. Variants end up in a VCF file. Depth is the number of reads covering a site; if reads are placed randomly, depth is Poisson distributed, so even at mean depth 8 some sites have no reads. At low depth a heterozygote may show only one allele, so we work with genotype likelihoods rather than hard calls.

Now the computational core. We need to place millions of reads on a three-gigabase genome. Dynamic programming per read against the whole genome is impossible, so we index the reference once and reuse that index for all reads. Hash tables and suffix trees work but need too much memory; the solution in BWA and Bowtie is the Burrows–Wheeler transform.

Construction: add a dollar sign, which is smaller than all letters, write all cyclic rotations of the text, sort them, and take the last column. Let me do BANANA.

The BWT is the last column: ANNB dollar AA. Equivalently, it is the character that precedes each suffix in sorted order — so it is closely related to the suffix array. Why does it help compression? Rows are sorted by what follows, so characters that precede the same context end up next to each other. In BANANA, both suffixes starting with "ANA" are preceded by N, giving the run NN. In a genome with repeats we get long runs of identical letters, which compress well with run-length encoding. The transform itself does not shorten the string, and it is lossless: we can invert it.

The key to inversion and search is the LF mapping: the $k$-th occurrence of a letter in the last column corresponds to the $k$-th occurrence of that letter in the first column — it is the same character of the text. We need two things: $C[c]$, the number of characters in the text smaller than $c$, and $Occ(c,i)$, the number of times $c$ occurs in the BWT up to row $i$.

$$
LF(i)=C[L[i]]+Occ(L[i],i),\qquad C:\ \$\!=\!0,\ A\!=\!1,\ B\!=\!4,\ N\!=\!5
$$

To decode, start in row 1, the row starting with dollar. Its last letter, A, is the last letter of the text. LF of 1 is $C[A]$ plus 1, so row 2, whose last letter is N. From row 2 we go to row 6 — A; then row 3 — N; row 7 — A; row 4 — B. Reading backwards, B-A-N-A-N-A. So we recover the text from the BWT alone.

The FM-index is the BWT plus the $C$ table, the $Occ$ structure, and a sample of suffix array positions. It needs around half a byte per base — about 1.5 gigabytes for the human genome, compared with about 12 for a suffix array and 45 for a suffix tree. Exact search is done backwards: we process the query from right to left and keep an interval of rows whose suffixes start with the part of the query read so far.

$$
\text{start: }[\,C[c]+1,\ C[c+1]\,],\qquad l'=C[c]+Occ(c,l-1)+1,\quad r'=C[c]+Occ(c,r)
$$

For the query ANA: the last letter A gives rows 2 to 4. Prepending N: $l = 5 + Occ(N,1) + 1 = 6$, $r = 5 + Occ(N,4) = 7$; rows 6 and 7 are NA dollar and NANA dollar. Prepending A: $l = 1 + Occ(A,5) + 1 = 3$, $r = 1 + Occ(A,7) = 4$. The final interval, rows 3 to 4, has size two, so ANA occurs twice, and the suffix array gives positions 4 and 2. If the interval ever becomes empty, there is no match. The time is proportional to the query length — independent of the genome size. The lecture example AGACCCATGACC with query GACC works the same way and ends with interval 11 to 12: two occurrences, at positions 2 and 9.

Reads, however, contain sequencing errors and real variants, so exact search alone is not enough. Mappers use seed and extend. BWA aln seeds with the first part of the read, around 32 bases, and allows a few differences by backtracking in the index — good for short reads, and preferred for ancient DNA. BWA mem finds many maximal exact matches along the read, clusters them, and extends with local Smith–Waterman alignment — better for longer reads. Reads in repeats get low mapping quality; paired-end information and longer reads help.

To finish with limitations: the reference must be close to the sample; repeats and structural variants are hard for short reads; duplicates and low library complexity inflate depth without adding information; and for ancient DNA, post-mortem deamination produces C-to-T changes at read ends, which look like mutations but also authenticate the data. *This topic connects to string matching, where suffix arrays were introduced, and to pairwise alignment, which is used in the extension step.*

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: collapse

- ★ What is the difference between Illumina and Nanopore? Advantages and disadvantages?

  Illumina (2nd gen): sequencing by synthesis on clonal clusters (bridge PCR), fluorescent reversible terminators imaged each cycle; 50–300 bp, paired-end; very accurate (~0.1% errors, mostly substitutions), enormous throughput, cheap per base. Disadvantages: short reads can't resolve repeats/structural variants or phase haplotypes; PCR/GC bias; long run times; big instruments. Nanopore (3rd gen): single molecule passing through a protein pore, current changes decoded; reads from kb to > 1 Mb, real time, portable (MinION), no amplification, direct RNA and base modifications. Disadvantages: higher error rate (indels, homopolymers), lower per-base accuracy, lower throughput per flow cell, more expensive per base.

- ★ What was there before NGS?

  Sanger sequencing (dideoxy chain termination; later capillary electrophoresis with fluorescent ddNTPs): one template per reaction, ~700–1000 bp reads, ~99.99% accuracy, low throughput, high cost per base — used for the Human Genome Project. (Still used for validating single loci.) Then 454 pyrosequencing (2005) started NGS.

- ★ What is the difference between a fragment and a read?

  Fragment (insert): the physical DNA molecule in the library, ligated between adapters. Read: the base sequence the instrument reports from one end (single-end) or both ends (paired-end) of a fragment, limited by read length. A 500-bp fragment with 2×150 PE reads leaves 200 bp unsequenced in the middle; a 40-bp aDNA fragment with 150-bp reads gives adapter read-through and fully overlapping pairs that are trimmed and merged. Insert size is estimated from the mapped pair distance.

- ★ Give a full BWT walkthrough including decoding.

  BANANA\$: rotations sorted → `$BANANA, A$BANAN, ANA$BAN, ANANA$B, BANANA$, NA$BANA, NANA$BA`; L = ANNB\$AA, F = \$AAABNN, SA = 7 6 4 2 1 5 3. C: \$0 A1 B4 N5. Decoding with $LF(i)=C[L[i]]+Occ(L[i],i)$ from row 1: L[1] = A → row 2 (N) → row 6 (A) → row 3 (N) → row 7 (A) → row 4 (B) → stop; read backwards BANANA. Search ANA: [2,4] → [6,7] → [3,4] → SA → 4 and 2.

- ★ Why does the BWT allow compression? Is it lossy?

  Sorting rotations groups together characters that are followed by the same context, so repeated substrings produce runs of the same preceding character (NN, AA in BANANA; long runs in repetitive genomes). Runs compress well with run-length or move-to-front + entropy coding. The BWT itself has the same length — it is a permutation — and it is lossless: it can be inverted exactly with LF mapping (needs the \$ / start row).

- ★ Walk through an ancient DNA workflow.

  Clean-lab sampling (petrous bone/teeth), contamination control → extraction (short fragments retained) → library building (adapters with indexes; optionally USER enzyme to remove uracils) → amplification, Illumina shotgun sequencing (PE) → QC, adapter trimming and merging of overlapping read pairs (fastp) → mapping with BWA aln (short reads; relaxed settings) → sort, mark/remove PCR duplicates (samtools fixmate/markdup) → filter by MAPQ and length (CIGAR) → authenticate: mapDamage (C→T at 5′, G→A at 3′), fragment length, contamination estimates (mtDNA, X in males) → genotype likelihoods / pseudo-haploid calls → population genetics. Complexity limits how much useful data you can get; endogenous % determines cost.

- ★ Where does reverse transcriptase come in?

  Sequencers (Illumina) read DNA; to sequence RNA (RNA-seq, RNA viruses) you first reverse-transcribe RNA into cDNA, then build a DNA library. Nanopore can additionally sequence native RNA directly without reverse transcription. Ancient DNA itself needs no reverse transcription.

- ★ When would you use 2nd-generation vs 3rd-generation sequencing?

  2nd gen: high-accuracy SNP/genotype calling, large cohorts, high depth at low cost, expression counting, targeted panels, ChIP-seq, and short/degraded DNA (aDNA, cfDNA) where long reads bring nothing. 3rd gen: de novo genome assembly, repetitive regions, structural variants, phasing, full-length transcripts/isoforms, methylation, rapid/field diagnostics (outbreaks). Often hybrid: long reads for scaffold, short reads for polishing.

- ★ Explain the sequencing process in general (Illumina).

  Fragment DNA → end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation (with index) → load on flow cell, bridge amplification makes clonal clusters → each cycle adds one fluorescent reversible-terminator nucleotide, image, cleave terminator/dye → base calling with quality scores → demultiplex by index → FASTQ. Paired-end: re-synthesise and read from the other end.

- Phred score: what is Q30? How is it stored?

  $Q=-10\log_{10}P(\text{error})$; Q30 = 1/1000 error. Stored as ASCII character with offset 33 (Q30 = '?').

- Base quality vs mapping quality?

  Base quality: probability the base call is wrong (from the sequencer). MAPQ: Phred-scaled probability that the read is placed at the wrong genomic position (from the aligner) — low for reads in repeats even if base qualities are perfect.

- What does the CIGAR string 5M1I4M2D3M mean?

  5 aligned (match/mismatch), 1 insertion in the read, 4 aligned, 2 deleted from the read relative to the reference, 3 aligned: consumes 13 read bases and 14 reference bases. S = soft clip, N = skipped region (introns in RNA-seq).

- Why is depth Poisson distributed, and what does this imply?

  If reads start independently and uniformly, the count covering a site is binomial with many trials and small probability → Poisson(mean depth). $P(D=0)=e^{-\lambda}$: at 1× 37% of sites uncovered; at 8× still a few with 0–2 reads; to cover all sites you need very high depth. Duplicates, GC bias and mappability make reality over-dispersed.

- Allele, genotype, genotype calling? Why not 50:50 for a heterozygote?

  Allele = variant form at a locus; genotype = the individual's alleles (e.g. A/G); calling = inferring it from data. Reads sample the two chromosomes randomly with replacement, so with depth 4 a heterozygote shows e.g. 3:1 or even 4:0 (prob $2\cdot0.5^4=12.5\%$ to see only one allele) — hence genotype likelihoods.

- Library complexity vs clonality?

  Complexity = number of unique molecules; clonality = fraction of duplicate reads. Sequencing deeper into a low-complexity library just re-reads duplicates (curve of new reads vs generated reads flattens).

- How do BWA aln and BWA mem differ?

  aln: backtracking search of the read prefix (seed ~32 bp) in the BWT allowing a few mismatches/gaps, bounded by a lower bound $D(i)$ on differences; good for short reads (< 70 bp, aDNA). mem: finds super-maximal exact matches as seeds, chains them, extends with banded Smith–Waterman; handles longer reads, local alignment, split reads.

- Why not use a hash table or suffix tree for the human genome?

  Suffix tree ~45 GB; suffix array ~12 GB; FM-index ~1.5 GB with $O(m)$ search; hash tables of $k$-mers need positions for every $k$-mer (several GB) and fixed $k$. The FM-index gives the best memory/speed trade-off.

- What does C[a] and Occ(a,i) mean?

  C[a]: number of characters in the text smaller than a (= row before the first row starting with a). Occ(a, i): how many a's are in L[1..i]. Together they give the LF mapping.

- What are the characteristics of aDNA damage and how is it useful?

  Deamination of cytosine to uracil, especially in single-stranded overhangs → C→T at 5′ ends and G→A at 3′ ends of reads; plus short fragments. These patterns distinguish authentic old molecules from modern contamination, but must be handled (trimming, rescaling qualities) to avoid false variants.
