## Finding Functional Sites with a Motif Model {#why-a-consensus-is-not-enough}

Examples are donor splice sites, transcription-factor binding sites and signal-peptide cleavage sites. Splicing removes introns from pre-mRNA and joins exons; the **donor** is the exon-to-intron boundary.

A **consensus** keeps the most frequent symbol at each position; a probability matrix preserves the alternatives and their frequencies. In the lecture, the most common complete sequence accounts for only 92 of 1230 donor sites (7.4%). Sites are aligned over 2 bases upstream and 6 downstream; the intronic GT is conserved, but GT alone is common in background DNA.

**Signal-peptide cleavage**: an N-terminal signal directs protein targeting and is then cleaved. The lecture aligns 1011 proteins over 10 residues before and 5 after the cleavage site, builds a $20 \times 15$ matrix, scans candidate positions and predicts the highest-scoring cleavage location. A realistic amino-acid background improves discrimination.

## Scanning a Sequence and Reading the Score {#scoring-windows}

Slide a width-$L$ matrix over a length-$n$ sequence: there are $n - L + 1$ windows (the lecture's 24-base example gives 17 eight-base windows). Add one matrix entry per position; peaks identify candidate sites. Naive scanning costs $O(nL)$.

Logs convert products into sums. ==A score $S$ means the window is $2^S$ times as probable under the motif model as under the background.== This is a likelihood ratio, not a posterior probability of function; posterior inference also needs prior probabilities.

## Choosing a Threshold {#thresholds-and-the-base-rate-problem}

Predict a site if $S \ge t$. Lowering $t$ increases sensitivity and false positives; raising $t$ reduces false positives but increases false negatives. Evaluate on held-out data or by cross-validation.

**Base-rate problem**: most genomic windows are negatives. With prevalence $\rho$,

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\rho\,\text{TPR}}{\rho\,\text{TPR} + (1-\rho)\,\text{FPR}},
$$

so even a small false-positive rate can yield many false alarms. The lecture's donor model gives about 1.5 false positives per kb at 80% sensitivity (dataset-specific). A high score identifies a candidate, not proof of function.

**Decision**: use a low threshold when candidates are filtered by further evidence (conservation, expression, a gene model), and a high one when follow-up is costly.

## Reading Sequence Logos {#reading-a-logo}

- Horizontal axis: aligned motif position.
- Total stack height: information content $I_i$.
- Relative letter heights: position-specific frequencies $p_i(a)$.
- Absolute letter height: $h_i(a) = p_i(a)\, I_i$.

Tall stacks indicate informative positions; the largest letter is the most frequent symbol. A zero-height stack under a uniform background means equal symbol frequencies, not an absent position. Do not confuse the logo height $p_i(a) I_i$ with the signed term $p_i(a) \log_2[p_i(a)/q(a)]$ in the KL sum.

::: table align="center" title="Entropy and information content of DNA columns"

| Column distribution | Entropy $H_i$ | Information $I_i$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Four equally frequent bases | 2 bits | 0 bits |
| Two equally frequent bases | 1 bit | 1 bit |
| One completely conserved base | 0 bits | 2 bits |

:::

## Limitations {#protein-application-and-limitations}

- Independent positions miss residue correlations.
- Fixed-width matrices do not explicitly model indels or variable spacing.
- Predictions depend on alignment quality, representative training data and the background.
- Local sequence alone does not capture all biological context, such as chromatin or co-factors.

Models that address some of these limits are hidden Markov models and neural networks.

## Building the Matrix: Counts, Probabilities, Log-odds {#counts-probabilities-and-log-odds-weights}

For $N$ aligned sites of width $L$, let $n_i(a)$ count symbol $a$ at position $i$:

$$
p_i(a) = \frac{n_i(a)}{N}, \qquad \sum_a p_i(a) = 1, \qquad w_i(a) = \log_2 \frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}.
$$

$q(a)$ is the background frequency. The counts form a **count matrix**, the $p_i(a)$ a **probability matrix**, and the $w_i(a)$ a **position-specific scoring matrix** (PSSM). PWM terminology varies, so state which representation is used.

Weights: positive = enriched relative to background; zero = equally frequent; negative = depleted. A uniform DNA background has $q(a) = 1/4$. Real nucleotide and amino-acid backgrounds may be unequal, which changes scores and predictions: with a wrong uniform background, symbols that are simply common everywhere are rewarded at every position and inflate background scores.

**Pseudocounts.** $p_i(a) = 0$ gives $w_i(a) = -\infty$, so any window with that symbol scores $-\infty$. An unobserved event in a small sample need not be impossible. Laplace smoothing adds one to each count:

$$
\hat p_i(a) = \frac{n_i(a) + 1}{N + 4}.
$$

Use $N + 20$ for proteins. Add pseudocounts before normalisation and logarithms.

## Window Score and Information Content {#information-content-is-the-expected-weight}

Under independent motif positions and an independent background,

$$
P(x \mid M) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p_i(x_i), \qquad P(x \mid B) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} q(x_i), \qquad
S(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{L} w_i(x_i) = \log_2 \frac{P(x \mid M)}{P(x \mid B)}.
$$

> **Say:** A logo summarises a column, whereas a PSSM scores a particular base. To connect them, average the score over the motif's base frequencies.

$$
I_i = \sum_a p_i(a)\, w_i(a) = \sum_a p_i(a) \log_2 \frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_i \,\|\, q).
$$

Relative entropy measures departure from the background, in bits. $I_i \ge 0$, with equality exactly when $p_i = q$. Individual weights may be negative although their motif-weighted average is non-negative. Total motif information is $\sum_i I_i$.

For a uniform background over $K$ symbols,

$$
I_i = \log_2 K - H_i, \qquad H_i = -\sum_a p_i(a) \log_2 p_i(a).
$$

For DNA, $I_i = 2 - H_i$ (take $0 \log 0 = 0$). The 2-bit maximum assumes a uniform DNA background. A fully conserved base $a$ against a non-uniform background gives $I_i = \log_2[1/q(a)]$. For a uniform protein background the maximum is $\log_2 20 \approx 4.32$ bits.

**Whiteboard calculation.** Let $p_i(G) = 0.7$, the other bases 0.1, and $q(a) = 0.25$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_i(G) &= \log_2 2.8 \approx 1.49, \qquad w_i(A, C, T) = \log_2 0.4 \approx -1.32, \\
I_i &= 0.7(1.49) + 3(0.1)(-1.32) \approx 0.64 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The G letter occupies $0.7\, I_i \approx 0.45$ bits. ==Distinguish the base weight (1.49), the column information (0.64) and the logo letter height (0.45).==

::: tip Whiteboard route

Known sites and question → scan and score windows → threshold and false positives → read the logo → support: counts → pseudocounts → log-odds → expected score / KL.

:::

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

**What it is for.** Suppose I want to predict where the donor splice sites are in a newly sequenced gene, or where a transcription factor binds in a promoter. Such sites are short and recognisable, but the individual instances are not identical. In the lecture's collection of donor sites, the single most common sequence accounts for only about seven percent of the sites. So a consensus string would miss most real sites. A weight matrix solves this: it describes, position by position, how likely each nucleotide is, and it lets me score any candidate sequence.

**Using the matrix: scanning.** I slide a window of the motif width along the sequence. For each window I look up one number per position in the matrix, the weight of the base that is actually there, and I add them. A sequence of length $n$ and a motif of width $L$ give $n - L + 1$ windows. The windows with the highest sums are my candidate sites. This is the whole method from the user's point of view: a table lookup and a sum.

**What the score means.** Each weight is a log-odds, $w_i(a) = \log_2 \frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}$: the probability of base $a$ at position $i$ of real sites, divided by its background probability. So the window score is the logarithm of a likelihood ratio: a score of $S$ means the window is $2^S$ times more probable under the motif model than under the background model. A score of zero means the two models explain the window equally well. It is important to say what the score is not: it is not the probability that the site is functional. For that I would also need the prior probability of a site, and real sites are very rare among all windows.

**Choosing a threshold.** I call a site when the score exceeds a threshold. A low threshold finds most real sites but also many false ones; a high threshold is clean but misses weak real sites. The base rate makes this hard: in a genome almost every window is a non-site, so even a small false-positive rate produces many false predictions. For the donor-site model in the lecture, a threshold that finds eighty percent of the true sites still gives about one and a half false positives per thousand bases. So in practice a matrix hit is a candidate. I choose a low threshold if other evidence will filter the candidates later, such as conservation, expression data or a gene model, and a high threshold if each prediction is expensive to follow up. The threshold should be evaluated on data that were not used to build the matrix.

**Building the matrix.** I start from known sites aligned without gaps. For each position I count the bases, which gives a count matrix; dividing by the number of sites gives a probability matrix; taking the log-ratio to the background gives the scoring matrix, the PSSM. One problem must be handled: if a base was never observed at a position, its probability is zero and its weight is minus infinity, so any window with that base is rejected however good the rest is. With a small sample, not seen does not mean impossible. So I add pseudocounts before normalising, for example one to each count, which gives $(n_i(a) + 1)/(N + 4)$ for DNA.

**Reading a sequence logo.** A logo is a picture of the same matrix. At each position there is a stack of letters. The total height of the stack is the information content of that position in bits, and within the stack each letter's share is its frequency. For DNA with a uniform background the information is 2 minus the entropy of the column: a position where all four bases are equally frequent has height zero, a position with two equally frequent bases has one bit, and a fully conserved position has two bits. So tall stacks are the positions that matter for recognition, and a flat position is not a missing position, just an uninformative one.

**The link between logo and matrix.** The information content of a column is the average weight I expect to collect at that position when I score a real site: $I_i = \sum_a p_i(a) \log_2 \frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}$. This is the relative entropy between the motif column and the background. It is never negative, although individual weights can be. A small example: if G has frequency 0.7 and the other bases 0.1 each, the weight of G is $\log_2 2.8 \approx 1.49$, the weight of the others is $\log_2 0.4 \approx -1.32$, and the information is $0.7 \times 1.49 - 0.3 \times 1.32 \approx 0.64$ bits. In the logo the letter G then has height $0.7 \times 0.64 \approx 0.45$ bits. These are three different numbers: the weight of a base, the information of a column, and the height of a letter.

**Limitations.** The matrix assumes that positions are independent, so it cannot represent the fact that a certain base at one position goes together with a certain base at another. It has a fixed width and no gaps. Its quality depends on the training sites being correctly aligned and representative, and on a sensible background. And a sequence signal alone does not capture the cellular context, such as chromatin or the presence of other factors. Models that address some of these limits are hidden Markov models and neural networks.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- ★ Why not just use the consensus sequence? What is the advantage of a weight matrix?

  A consensus keeps only the most frequent base per position and throws away how strongly each position is conserved and which alternatives are tolerated. Most real sites differ from the consensus somewhere (donor sites: the best sequence is only 7.4% of sites), so exact matching misses them, and it cannot rank near-matches. A weight matrix gives a graded log-likelihood score, weights positions by importance, and allows a threshold to trade sensitivity against specificity.

- ★ A window scores 6 bits. How do you interpret positive and negative scores?

  The window is $2^6 = 64$ times more likely under the motif model than under the background. A positive weight means the base is enriched at that position, a negative weight depleted, zero uninformative. It does not say the site is real with high probability: if real sites are, say, one in ten thousand windows, most windows with this score are still false.

- ★ Why are pseudocounts needed and when do they matter most?

  To avoid zero probabilities, which give minus infinity and veto a whole window because of one unseen base. They matter most when the number of training sites is small; with many sites the counts dominate and the pseudocount has little effect.

- ★ In a logo, what do the total height and the relative letter heights show?

  The total height of a stack is the information content of that position, $I_i = \sum_a p_i(a)\log_2\frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}$; the relative heights of the letters are their frequencies at that position. The absolute height of a letter is frequency times information. $I_i$ is also the average PSSM score collected at that position by real sites.

- What is the maximum information per position for DNA and for protein?

  With a uniform background, $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits for DNA and $\log_2 20 \approx 4.32$ bits for protein. With a non-uniform background a fully conserved rare base gives more than a common one, $\log_2[1/q(a)]$.

- ★ What if the background is not uniform? Why does its accuracy matter?

  Then use the real composition $q(a)$ of the genome or proteome. The log-odds measures enrichment relative to background; with a wrong uniform background, symbols that are common everywhere (A/T in AT-rich genomes, Leu in proteins) are rewarded at every position, inflating the scores of background sequence and increasing false positives. The lecture showed that signal-peptide discrimination improves with a realistic amino-acid background.

- Your matrix predicts thousands of sites in a genome. How do you deal with that?

  I expect most of them to be false because of the low base rate. I would raise the threshold, estimate the false-positive rate on background or shuffled sequence, and combine the score with independent evidence: conservation across species, position relative to genes, experimental data, or a model that puts the site into context.

- What is the main assumption of a weight matrix, and when does it fail?

  Independence between positions. It fails when positions are correlated, for example when a weak base at one position is compensated by a strong one at another, and when sites have variable spacing.

:::
