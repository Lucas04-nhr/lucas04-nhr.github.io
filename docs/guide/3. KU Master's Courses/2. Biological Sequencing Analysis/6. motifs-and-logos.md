Motifs are short, variable, functional patterns. This topic follows the path from aligned sites to a position-specific scoring matrix, how to scan with it and set thresholds, and how information content connects the matrix to a sequence logo.

## Why a Consensus is Not Enough {#why-a-consensus-is-not-enough}

A sequence motif is a short recurring pattern associated with a function: donor splice sites, transcription-factor binding sites, signal-peptide cleavage sites. The donor site is the exon-to-intron boundary in pre-mRNA. Instances vary, so a consensus loses information. In the course's donor-site data, the single most common complete sequence accounted for only 92 of 1230 sites (about 7.5%). The intronic GT is conserved, but GT alone is common in background DNA.

Splicing removes introns from pre-mRNA: the **donor** site is the exon→intron boundary (the intron starts with GT) and the **acceptor** the intron→exon boundary (the intron ends with AG). The donor data set uses 2 bp of exon and 6 bp of intron; the ten most common complete sequences cover only just over half of the sites. GT alone is useless as a predictor because it occurs every 16 bp in random DNA ($p = 1/16$).

Compared with a consensus, a weight matrix keeps the frequency of every letter at every position (alternatives and degree of conservation), gives a graded score rather than match/no match, can be thresholded to trade sensitivity for specificity, and has a probabilistic interpretation.

## Counts, Probabilities and Log-odds Weights {#counts-probabilities-and-log-odds-weights}

For $N$ aligned sites of width $L$, with $n_i(a)$ the count of symbol $a$ at position $i$ and background $q(a)$:

$$
p_i(a) = \frac{n_i(a)}{N}, \qquad w_i(a) = \log_2 \frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}
$$

The terminology varies (count matrix, probability matrix, PWM, PSSM), so it is worth stating which one is meant. Positive weights mean enrichment relative to background, negative weights depletion. A uniform DNA background is $q(a) = 1/4$, but real backgrounds are often unequal and change the scores.

An unobserved symbol gives $p_i(a) = 0$ and $w_i(a) = -\infty$, which would veto any window containing it. Pseudocounts fix this; Laplace smoothing adds one per symbol before normalising:

$$
p_i(a) = \frac{n_i(a) + 1}{N + 4} \quad (\text{DNA}), \qquad \frac{n_i(a)+1}{N + 20} \quad (\text{protein})
$$

The background matters: genomes can be AT-rich and the human proteome is not uniform (leucine is common, tryptophan rare). With a wrong (uniform) background, letters that are common everywhere are rewarded at every position, which inflates scores of background sequence and increases false positives. Background-proportional pseudocounts $\frac{n_i(a) + A q(a)}{N + A}$ are an alternative to Laplace smoothing. For example, with 1230 sites and a G counted 1220 times, Laplace gives $1221/1234 = 0.989$ and a weight of $\log_2(0.989/0.25) = 1.98$ bits; a base seen twice gives $3/1234 = 0.0024$ and $-6.7$ bits.

## Scoring Windows {#scoring-windows}

Assuming independent positions under both models:

$$
S(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{L} w_i(x_i) = \log_2 \frac{P(x \mid M)}{P(x \mid B)}
$$

A score $S$ means the window is $2^S$ times as probable under the motif model as under background. This is a likelihood ratio, not a posterior probability of function, which would also need a prior. Sliding a width-$L$ matrix over a length-$n$ sequence gives $n - L + 1$ windows (24 bases and an 8-base matrix give 17 windows) at a naive cost of $O(nL)$.

## Thresholds and the Base-rate Problem {#thresholds-and-the-base-rate-problem}

Predict a site when $S \ge t$. Lowering $t$ raises sensitivity and false positives; raising it does the opposite. Thresholds should be evaluated on held-out data or by cross-validation. Because most genomic windows are negatives, precision with prevalence $\pi$ is

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\pi\,\text{TPR}}{\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}}
$$

so even a small false-positive rate can swamp true hits. The course's donor model gave roughly 1.5 false positives per kilobase at 80% sensitivity, a dataset-specific figure.

The same point in Bayesian form: the posterior probability that a window with score $S$ is a real site, given a prior fraction $\pi$ of real sites, is

$$
P(\text{site} \mid x) = \frac{2^S \pi}{2^S \pi + (1 - \pi)},
$$

so a tiny $\pi$ keeps the posterior modest even for good scores.

## Information Content is the Expected Weight {#information-content-is-the-expected-weight}

A logo summarises a column, while a PSSM scores a base. Averaging the weight over the motif frequencies connects them:

$$
I_i = \sum_a p_i(a)\, w_i(a) = \sum_a p_i(a) \log_2 \frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_i \,\|\, q)
$$

Individual weights can be negative, but $I_i \ge 0$, with equality only when $p_i = q$. For a uniform background over $K$ symbols, $I_i = \log_2 K - H_i$ with $H_i = -\sum_a p_i(a) \log_2 p_i(a)$; for DNA, $I_i = 2 - H_i$.

::: table align="center" title="Entropy and information content of DNA columns"

| Column distribution | $H_i$ | $I_i$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Four equally frequent bases | 2 bits | 0 bits |
| Two equally frequent bases | 1 bit | 1 bit |
| One fully conserved base | 0 bits | 2 bits |

:::

The 2-bit ceiling assumes a uniform background; a fully conserved base $a$ against a non-uniform background gives $\log_2[1/q(a)]$. For proteins the uniform maximum is $\log_2 20 \approx 4.32$ bits.

## Reading a Logo {#reading-a-logo}

The horizontal axis is motif position, the total stack height is $I_i$, relative letter heights are the frequencies, and the absolute letter height is $h_i(a) = p_i(a)\, I_i$. A zero-height stack under a uniform background means equal frequencies, not a missing position. Logo heights should not be confused with the signed terms inside the KL sum.

**Small calculation.** With $p_i(\text{G}) = 0.7$, other bases 0.1 and $q = 0.25$:

$$
w_i(\text{G}) = \log_2 2.8 \approx 1.49, \quad w_i(\text{A,C,T}) = \log_2 0.4 \approx -1.32, \quad
I_i \approx 0.7(1.49) + 0.3(-1.32) \approx 0.64 \text{ bits}
$$

The G letter is $0.7 \times 0.64 \approx 0.45$ bits tall. Three different numbers describe the same column: base weight 1.49, column information 0.64, and letter height 0.45.

A letter can have a large weight but a small logo height: a rare letter that is slightly enriched has a high $w_i(a)$ but a small $p_i(a)$, and logo heights use frequency times information, not weights. A fully conserved base gives 2 bits because knowing it resolves a four-way uncertainty, i.e. two binary questions.

## Protein Application and Limitations {#protein-application-and-limitations}

For signal-peptide cleavage, 1011 proteins were aligned over 10 residues before and 5 after the cut, giving a $20 \times 15$ matrix used to scan candidate positions with a realistic amino-acid background. The model's limits are structural: independent positions ignore residue correlations, fixed widths do not model indels, and predictions depend on alignment quality and representative training data.

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Keyword Card {#keyword-card}

- **Motif**: short, recurring, variable, functional (donor GT, TFBS, RBS, signal peptide)
- **Donor**: 1230 sites, −2..+6; top seq 92/1230 = 7.4%; top 10 ≈ ½; GT every 16 bp → consensus fails
- **Counts → freq** $p=n/N$ → **PSSM** $w=\log_2\frac pq$; + up / 0 / − down
- **Pseudocounts**: $p=0$ → $-\infty$; Laplace $\frac{n+1}{N+4}$ (N+20 protein)
- **Background**: non-uniform (proteome), affects scores
- **Score** $S=\sum w_i(x_i)$ = log-likelihood ratio; $2^S$ times; not posterior
- **Scan**: $n-L+1$ windows (24 bp → 17); peaks
- **Threshold**: sens vs FP; 80% TP → 1.5 FP/kb; base rate; cross-validation
- **IC** $=\sum p\log_2\frac pq$ = KL ≥ 0 = mean score; DNA max 2 bits
- **Logo**: stack = IC, letter ∝ freq, height = $p\cdot I$
- **Example** 0.7/0.1: w 1.49 / −1.32; I 0.64; G 0.45

- **Limits**: independence, fixed width/no gaps, training data, background → discovery / pHMM

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

My topic is motifs, weight matrices and sequence logos. A motif is a short sequence pattern that recurs, with variation, and is associated with a biological function — for example splice sites, transcription-factor binding sites, ribosome binding sites, or the cleavage site of a signal peptide. The lecture example was the donor splice site: the boundary where an intron starts, after an exon; introns almost always start with GT.

The first question is how to describe such a pattern. The simplest description is a consensus sequence — the most common letter at each position. But in the data set of 1230 human donor sites, taking two bases of exon and six of intron, the single most common sequence occurs only 92 times, 7.4 percent, and the ten most common sequences cover only about half. So a consensus would miss most real sites. And GT alone is useless, because GT occurs every 16 bases in random DNA. We need a description that keeps the variation: a weight matrix.

We align the known sites and count each nucleotide at each position, which gives a count matrix; dividing by the number of sites gives a position-specific frequency matrix. To score sequences we compare with a background model: $q(a)$, the frequency of $a$ in sequences in general. The weight, or position-specific score, is the log-odds

$$
w_i(a)=\log_2\frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}
$$

and the table of these is the PSSM. A positive weight means the letter is more frequent at this position in real sites than in the background — up-weighted; zero means equally frequent; negative means less frequent than expected — down-weighted.

One problem: if a letter was never observed at a position, $p=0$ and the weight is minus infinity, so any sequence with that letter gets minus infinity, even if it is a perfectly good site. Not observing something in a small sample does not mean it is impossible. So we add pseudocounts before normalising — for example Laplace: add one to each count, $\frac{n+1}{N+4}$ for DNA. With lots of data the pseudocounts hardly matter.

The background also matters. A uniform background, one quarter for each base, is convenient, but genomes and proteomes are not uniform — in the human proteome leucine is common and tryptophan rare. With the wrong background, letters that are simply common everywhere get rewarded.

To score a candidate window, we add up the weights of its letters:

$$
S(x)=\sum_i w_i(x_i)=\log_2\frac{P(x\mid\text{motif})}{P(x\mid\text{background})}
$$

Because of the logs, the sum equals the log-likelihood ratio of the motif model versus the background model, assuming independent positions. A score of $S$ bits means the window is $2^S$ times more likely under the motif model. It is not the probability that the site is real — that would also need a prior. To scan a sequence, we slide the window along it; a sequence of length $n$ has $n-L+1$ windows — the 24-base exercise sequence gives 17 eight-base windows — and peaks in the score profile are candidate sites.

To classify, we choose a threshold. A lower threshold catches more true sites but gives more false positives; a higher threshold the opposite. The lecture showed the bad news: because GT dinucleotides are so much more common than real donor sites, predicting 80 percent of true donors gives about 1.5 false positives per kilobase. That is the base-rate problem: a small false-positive rate times a huge number of candidates gives many false alarms. Performance must be evaluated on data not used for training, for example with cross-validation.

Now, how much information is there in each position? We take the average score at a position, weighted by the motif frequencies:

$$
I_i=\sum_a p_i(a)\log_2\frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}
$$

This is the relative entropy, or Kullback–Leibler divergence, between the position's distribution and the background. It is always at least zero, zero exactly when the position looks like background, and with log base two it is measured in bits; for DNA with uniform background the maximum is 2 bits — a completely conserved position. That is the information content.

A sequence logo shows this. The horizontal axis is the position; the total height of each stack is the information content, so conserved positions are tall; within the stack each letter's height is proportional to its frequency, so its absolute height is $p_i(a)$ times $I_i$, with the most frequent letter on top. In the donor logo, the G and T of the intron are two bits tall, and the surrounding positions are much lower.

A small example: if G has frequency 0.7 at a position and the other three bases 0.1 each, with a uniform background, the weight of G is log2 of 2.8, about 1.49 bits, the other weights are −1.32, the information content of the column is 0.64 bits, and the G in the logo is 0.7 times 0.64, about 0.45 bits tall.

So we have three different numbers: the weight of G, 1.49; the information of the column, 0.64 bits; and the height of the G letter, 0.45.

The same method works for proteins: for signal peptides, 1011 eukaryotic proteins aligned at the cleavage site, 10 residues before and 5 after, give a 20 by 15 matrix; scoring all positions and predicting the highest one finds the cleavage site, and a realistic amino-acid background improves discrimination.

The limitations: positions are assumed independent, so dependencies between positions are ignored; the motif has a fixed width without insertions or deletions; results depend on the training alignment and the background; and a high score indicates a candidate, not a functional site. When the motif itself is unknown we need motif discovery, and when we need gaps we move to profile HMMs.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- ★ Explain the height of a stack in the logo / information content.

  Stack height at position $i$ = information content $I_i=\sum_ap_i(a)\log_2\frac{p_i(a)}{q(a)}$, the relative entropy between the position's distribution and the background, in bits. With uniform DNA background, $I_i=2-H_i$: 2 bits for a fully conserved base, 0 when all four are equally frequent. Within a stack, letter heights are proportional to frequencies ($p_i(a)\cdot I_i$), most frequent on top. It is also the average PSSM score of motif instances at that position.

- ★ Why pseudocounts?

  With few observed sites, unobserved letters get $p=0$ → $w=-\infty$, so any candidate containing that letter is rejected regardless of everything else, and estimates are over-confident. Pseudocounts (Laplace +1, or background-proportional, or substitution-based for proteins) give small non-zero probabilities; their influence vanishes as data increase.

- ★ What is the advantage of a weight matrix compared to a consensus sequence?

  A consensus keeps only the most frequent letter, giving a yes/no match that misses most real sites (donor: best sequence 7.4% of sites) and ignores how conserved each position is. A weight matrix keeps all frequencies, weights positions by importance, gives a graded log-likelihood score, allows thresholds for sensitivity vs specificity, and corresponds to a probabilistic model; logos built from it show both conservation and alternatives.

- ★ What if the background is not uniform? Why does accuracy of the background matter?

  Then use the real composition $q(a)$ (genome/proteome). The log-odds measures enrichment relative to background; with a wrong (uniform) background, letters that are simply common everywhere (A/T in AT-rich genomes, Leu in proteins) are rewarded at every position, inflating scores of background sequence and increasing false positives; rare letters get under-rewarded. The lecture showed discrimination of signal peptides improves with a realistic amino-acid background. Information content also changes: max for letter $a$ becomes $\log_2(1/q(a))$.

- ★ Interpret positive and negative log scores.

  $w_i(a)>0$: letter $a$ is more frequent at this motif position than in background — supports the motif (up). $<0$: less frequent than expected (down) — evidence against. 0: uninformative. Total score $S$: the window is $2^S$ times more likely under the motif model than background; $S>0$ favours the motif, but because true sites are rare a threshold well above 0 is needed.

- How do you go from counts to a PSSM — calculate one entry.

  Illustrative numbers: 1230 sites, a position where G is counted 1220 times → with Laplace $(1220+1)/(1230+4)=0.989$ → $\log_2(0.989/0.25)=1.98$ bits. A base seen 2 times: $(2+1)/1234=0.0024$ → $\log_2(0.0097)=-6.7$.

- How many windows in a sequence? What is the cost of scanning?

  $n-L+1$ windows; each needs $L$ additions → $O(nL)$.

- Is a PSSM score a probability that the site is real?

  No, it is a log-likelihood ratio. The posterior $P(\text{site}|x)$ requires a prior: $\frac{2^S\pi}{2^S\pi+(1-\pi)}$ with $\pi$ = prior fraction of real sites — tiny, so even good scores can have modest posterior probability (base-rate problem).

- How do you choose a threshold and evaluate the matrix?

  Score known sites and non-sites (held-out / cross-validation), plot score histograms, choose a threshold by the desired sensitivity/false positive trade-off (ROC); report FP per kb. Never test on the training sites.

- Relationship between information content and the PSSM?

  $I_i$ = expected (average) PSSM weight under the motif distribution: $\sum_ap_i(a)w_i(a)$. Individual weights can be negative; the average is ≥ 0 (Gibbs' inequality).

- Main assumptions/limitations of weight matrices?

  Independence between positions (misses dependencies such as correlated positions in splice sites), fixed length and no gaps, requires aligned training sites, background model, ignores context (chromatin, cofactors). Fixes: higher-order/dependency models, HMMs, profile HMMs, neural networks.

- Logo heights vs PSSM weights — can a letter have a large weight but small logo height?

  Yes. A rare letter that is slightly enriched has a high weight but small frequency, so its height $p\cdot I$ is small; logos show frequency × information, not weights.

- Why does a fully conserved position give 2 bits?

  $\log_2(1/0.25)=2$: knowing the base removes a 4-way uncertainty, which takes 2 binary questions.

:::
