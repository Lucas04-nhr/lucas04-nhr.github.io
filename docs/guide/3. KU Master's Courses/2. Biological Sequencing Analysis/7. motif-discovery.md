In motif scanning the model is known and its occurrences are sought. In motif discovery an experiment gives a set of sequences believed to share a signal, and both the pattern and its locations are unknown. The page starts with which discovery method fits which data and how an enrichment result is read, then explains the statistics and the Gibbs sampler that support them.

::: tip Opening

Suppose an experiment gives me a set of genes that respond together, for example genes that go up when a microRNA is knocked down, or co-regulated genes in a bacterium. I suspect a shared sequence signal in their UTRs or promoters, but I know neither the pattern nor where it sits. I will start with which discovery method fits which data and how an enrichment result is read, and then explain the statistics and the Gibbs sampler that support them.

:::

## What Do These Sequences Have in Common {#scanning-versus-discovery}

Example: co-regulated genes may share a transcription-factor binding motif in their promoters.

- **Scanning**: the motif model is known; find its occurrences.
- **Discovery**: both the motif model and its locations are unknown. Short, variable motifs can occur by chance in a heterogeneous background.

::: table align="center" title="Choosing a discovery method"

| Data and expectation | Method |
| :--- | :--- |
| Foreground vs background; short exact signal (miRNA site) | Word counting + enrichment test |
| Genes ranked by expression change, no natural cutoff | Ranked enrichment (Sylamer, cWords) |
| Variable motif, one site per sequence (RBS, TF site) | PSSM by Gibbs sampling / MEME |

:::

## Expression Changes and Ranked Enrichment {#ranked-enrichment}

In a miR-21 knock-down, direct targets may increase in expression when repression is released, so their 3′ UTRs are searched for enriched targeting signals (the word complementary to the miRNA seed). A sequence hit alone does not prove direct targeting.

A fixed expression cutoff is arbitrary. Instead, rank genes by expression change and test enrichment across successive cutoffs. Sylamer uses Fisher statistics along ranked sequences; cWords uses a related ranking idea with different statistics.

## The Background Defines What Is Surprising {#why-a-markov-background-matters}

Neighbouring bases are correlated, so an independent model can mistake composition biases for motifs. A first-order Markov model uses

$$
P_0(w) = f(w_1) \prod_{i=2}^{k} P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}).
$$

**Lecture example**: 761 bases, 144 C, 178 G, 24 CG, 30 GC and one CGCG. Ignoring edge effects,

$$
P_0(\text{CGCG}) = \frac{144}{761} \cdot \frac{24}{144} \cdot \frac{30}{178} \cdot \frac{24}{144}.
$$

::: table align="center" title="CGCG under two backgrounds"

| Background | Expected count $\lambda$ | Enrichment $1/\lambda$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Independent bases | 1.49 | 0.67 |
| First-order Markov | 0.674 | 1.48 |

:::

==The same word changes from apparent depletion to enrichment: the background defines what is surprising.== Neither ratio alone establishes significance. A very high-order background has many parameters ($3 \cdot 4^m$ for order $m$) and, as its order approaches the motif length, starts to model the motif itself.

## Reading Results: Significance, Limitations and Checks {#significance}

An **enrichment ratio** says how large the departure is; a **$p$-value** says how surprising it is under the background model; testing all $4^k$ words requires **multiple-testing correction**.

**Fisher's exact test**: compare word hits and non-hits in foreground and background with a $2 \times 2$ table. Conditional on the margins, sum the hypergeometric tail for enrichment. Match composition, lengths and counting units; overlapping occurrences can violate independence. Estimate the background excluding the tested sequence.

Limitations of motif finders:

- Finite runs do not guarantee the best motif; sampling can remain around local solutions or shifted patterns.
- The width may be wrong; sequences may contain zero or several sites.
- Remedies: multiple starts, joint shifts, width changes, flexible site counts and better backgrounds. A standard PSSM cannot model dependencies between motif positions.

Lecture application: short upstream regions of *E. coli* genes, searching for a shared ribosome binding signal. An HMM can represent background and successive motif-position states, the hidden path specifying motif locations.

## Statistics for Word Enrichment {#word-enrichment}

Enumerate all $k$-mers ($4^k$ for DNA), count occurrences and compare with the background. For independent bases with frequencies $q(b)$,

$$
P_0(w) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} q(w_i), \qquad \lambda = M P_0(w), \qquad E = \frac{z}{\lambda}.
$$

$z$ is the observed count and $M = \sum_s \max(L_s - k + 1, 0)$ the number of valid starting positions (the slides approximate $M$ by the total length). $E > 1$ means enrichment relative to the chosen background, not statistical significance. For rare, approximately independent occurrences,

$$
K \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad p = P(K \ge z) = \sum_{j=z}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^j}{j!}.
$$

Self-overlapping or periodic words create dependent counts and can invalidate the approximation.

## Representing Variable Motifs with a PSSM {#pssm}

Exact-word methods split a variable motif into many strings; a probability matrix combines the alternatives. Assume independent motif positions with base probabilities $p_i(b)$. Against an independent background $q(b)$,

$$
w_i(b) = \log_2 \frac{p_i(b)}{q(b)}, \qquad S(x, a) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \log_2 \frac{p_i(x_{a+i-1})}{q(x_{a+i-1})}.
$$

$a$ is the candidate start and $k$ the motif width. A positive window score favours the motif over background. Pseudocounts prevent zero probabilities and $-\infty$ scores (see [Motifs, Weight Matrices and Logos](/docs/ku-bsa/motifs-and-logos/)).

## Gibbs Sampling: Learn Sites and the Motif {#variable-motifs-and-gibbs-sampling}

> **Say:** Known sites give us a matrix, and a known matrix lets us score sites. We break this circular problem by repeatedly updating one sequence while holding the others fixed.

Assume a fixed width, one ungapped site per sequence, and independent positions and background.

::: steps

1. Choose a random motif start in every sequence.
2. Select one sequence $r$ and temporarily exclude it.
3. Estimate the motif matrix from the selected sites in the remaining sequences, using pseudocounts.
4. Score every valid start in sequence $r$.
5. Convert scores into probabilities and sample a new start.
6. Repeat; assess stability and use multiple random starts.

:::

Leaving out $r$ prevents its current site from reinforcing its own rescoring matrix. Conditional updates avoid exhaustive enumeration of all site combinations.

### From Scores to Sampling Probabilities {#sampling-probabilities}

With a fixed background and a uniform prior over starts,

$$
P(x \mid a, W) = Q(x)\, 2^{S(x,a)}, \qquad P(a \mid x, W) = \frac{2^{S(x,a)}}{\sum_j 2^{S(x,j)}}.
$$

$Q(x)$ is the all-background probability, constant across candidate starts, so it cancels on normalisation. With natural-log scores, use $e^S$ instead.

==Sample according to the probabilities; do not always choose the maximum.== Lower-scoring starts remain possible, allowing exploration beyond the current preferred site.

**Whiteboard example**: scores $(0, 1, 2)$ give weights $(1, 2, 4)$ and probabilities $(1/7, 2/7, 4/7)$. Draw $u \sim U[0, 1)$ and select the interval containing $u$: $[0, 1/7)$, $[1/7, 3/7)$, $[3/7, 1)$. For $u = 0.6$, choose the third site.

## EM and MEME {#em-and-meme}

Instead of sampling one site per sequence, **expectation–maximisation** (EM) uses soft assignments:

- **E-step**: for every sequence and every start, compute the posterior probability that the motif starts there, $P(a \mid x, W) \propto 2^{S(x,a)}$.
- **M-step**: re-estimate the matrix from all windows weighted by these posteriors (expected counts), plus pseudocounts.

EM is deterministic for a given start and climbs to a local maximum, so it needs many starting points. **MEME** seeds EM from candidate words in the data, supports different site-occurrence models (one, zero-or-one, or any number of sites per sequence) and can discover several motifs. Local optima remain possible.

::: table align="center" title="Gibbs sampling, EM and Viterbi-style training"

| Method | Assignment of sites | Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Gibbs sampling | One sampled site per sequence (hard, stochastic) | Can escape local optima |
| EM / MEME | Posterior weights over all positions (soft) | Deterministic, local optimum |
| Viterbi-style training | Best site only (hard argmax) | Fast, greedy |

:::

Baum–Welch for HMMs ([Hidden Markov Models](/docs/ku-bsa/hidden-markov-models/)) is the same EM principle.

::: tip Whiteboard route

Data and question → choose method → background / CGCG example → significance and multiple testing → PSSM → leave-one-out Gibbs update → normalised weights / random draw.

Closing: an enriched motif is a candidate biological signal; enrichment alone does not prove binding or regulatory function.

:::

## Ten-Minute Presentation {#presentation}

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

**What it is for.** In the previous topic the motif was known and I looked for its occurrences. Here the situation is the reverse. An experiment gives me a set of sequences that I believe share a signal, for example the 3′ UTRs of genes that go up when a microRNA is knocked down, or the promoters of co-regulated genes. I know neither what the pattern looks like nor where it is. Motif discovery has to find both at once.

**Which method for which data.** There are two families. If the signal is short and nearly exact, like a microRNA seed site, I count words: every $k$-mer is counted in my sequences and compared with what I would expect. If the signal is variable, like a transcription-factor site or a ribosome binding site, exact words split it into many variants, so I learn a weight matrix instead, with Gibbs sampling or with MEME. And if my genes come as a ranked list, for example ordered by expression change, I use a method that tests enrichment along the ranking, such as Sylamer or cWords, so that I do not have to choose an arbitrary cutoff.

**Word counting.** For a word $w$ I compute its probability under a background model, multiply by the number of positions where it could occur, and get the expected count $\lambda$. If I observe $z$ occurrences, the ratio $z/\lambda$ is the enrichment. To judge whether the enrichment is significant, I can use the Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda$ and ask how probable it is to see $z$ or more occurrences. Because I test all $4^k$ words, I must correct for multiple testing. Alternatively I compare a foreground set with a background set of sequences with Fisher's exact test.

**The background decides what is surprising.** This is the most important practical point. If my background model assumes independent bases, any word that is common simply because of the dinucleotide composition of the genome will look enriched or depleted. A Markov background fixes this: in a first-order model the probability of each base depends on the previous one. The lecture example is the word CGCG in a sequence of 761 bases. With independent bases the expected count is about 1.5, so one observed occurrence looks like depletion. With a first-order Markov model, which knows that CG dinucleotides are rare, the expected count is about 0.67, and the same single occurrence now looks like enrichment. The data did not change; only my definition of normal did.

**The microRNA example.** When a microRNA is knocked down, its direct targets are no longer repressed, so their expression goes up. I rank all genes by expression change and ask which words are over-represented in the 3′ UTRs at the top of the list. If the word complementary to the seed of that microRNA comes out, this supports that the effect is direct. But a sequence match alone does not prove that a particular gene is a direct target.

**Gibbs sampling: the idea.** For a variable motif there is a circular problem. If I knew where the sites are, I could build the matrix. If I knew the matrix, I could find the sites. Gibbs sampling breaks the circle by iterating. I start with a random site in every sequence. Then I take one sequence out, build the matrix from the sites in all the other sequences, with pseudocounts, score every possible position in the sequence I left out, and choose a new site for it. I repeat this for sequence after sequence, many times. If a real motif is present, the matrix gradually becomes sharper, and the sharper matrix pulls more sites to the right position.

**Two details that make it work.** First, the sequence being updated is left out when the matrix is built, so that its current site cannot vote for itself. Second, the new site is sampled, not simply taken as the best one. The scores are turned into probabilities, $P(a) \propto 2^{S(a)}$, and I draw a position according to these probabilities. For example, three positions with scores 0, 1 and 2 get weights 1, 2 and 4, so probabilities 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7. The best position is the most likely choice, but the others remain possible, and this randomness is what allows the procedure to escape from a poor starting configuration.

**How I judge the result.** A motif finder always returns something, so the output is a hypothesis. I run it several times from different random starts and see whether the same motif comes back. I check for known problems: the sampler can lock onto a shifted version of the motif, the assumed width may be wrong, and the assumption of exactly one site per sequence may not hold. MEME addresses some of this by using expectation–maximisation and by allowing zero or several sites per sequence. Finally, an enriched motif is a candidate signal; showing that it is bound or functional needs independent evidence. In the lecture the method was applied to the regions upstream of *E. coli* genes, where it recovers the ribosome binding site.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- What is the difference between motif scanning and motif discovery?

  In scanning the model is given and I look for its occurrences. In discovery neither the model nor the positions are known; I only have a set of sequences assumed to share a signal.

- Why can the choice of background model change the conclusion?

  Because enrichment is always relative to an expectation. If the background ignores real properties of the genome, such as rare CG dinucleotides or A/T-rich UTRs, words that reflect these properties look significant. A higher-order Markov model captures them, as in the CGCG example where the expected count drops from about 1.5 to about 0.67.

- Why not always use a very high-order Markov background?

  The number of parameters grows as $4^m$ times three, so I need a lot of sequence to estimate them. And if the order approaches the motif length, the background starts to model the motif itself, and the motif no longer looks surprising.

- ★ Explain Gibbs sampling.

  An iterative stochastic search for the motif positions and the PSSM at the same time: random start sites → leave one sequence out → PSSM from the others with pseudocounts → score every window of the left-out sequence → probabilities $\propto 2^S$ → sample a new site → repeat until stable, with several restarts. It is heuristic, with no guarantee of the global optimum.

- ★ In Gibbs sampling, why sample the new position instead of taking the best one?

  Always taking the maximum is a greedy procedure that quickly gets stuck in the first pattern it finds, often a chance pattern from the random start. Sampling keeps exploring: good positions are chosen most often, but not always.

- ★ Why is the current sequence left out when the matrix is estimated?

  Otherwise its present site contributes to the matrix and then scores well against it, which reinforces the current choice regardless of whether it is right.

- How do you know that the motif you found is real?

  I check reproducibility over several random starts, compare its score with what I get on shuffled or unrelated sequences, see whether it resembles a known motif, and look for independent support such as conservation or binding data. Statistical enrichment alone shows association, not function.

- ★ What does MEME do differently from the Gibbs sampler? Explain the EM algorithm in this context.

  It uses expectation–maximisation: instead of sampling one site per sequence, the E-step weights every possible site by its posterior probability under the current model, and the M-step re-estimates the matrix from these weighted sites (expected counts plus pseudocounts), iterating until the likelihood converges. It is deterministic for a given start, tries many starting points, and supports models with zero, one or several sites per sequence. Baum–Welch is EM for HMMs.

:::
