Motif discovery starts from sequences that are believed to share a signal and looks for it without knowing either the pattern or its locations. The two families of methods are word-based enrichment (exhaustive) and weight-matrix estimation (Gibbs sampling, EM), and the background model decides what counts as surprising.

## Scanning versus Discovery {#scanning-versus-discovery}

Scanning assumes the motif model is known and looks for occurrences. Discovery starts from sequences believed to share a signal, such as promoters of co-regulated genes, without knowing either the pattern or its locations. Short, variable motifs can also arise by chance in heterogeneous background.

The typical motivation is a cluster of co-expressed genes whose promoters may share a transcription-factor binding site. Such sites carry little information, and factors bind in many places (c-Myc at roughly 25,000 sites), so scanning with a known matrix (from TRANSFAC or JASPAR) finds too many sites. The usual simplifying assumptions are: one (or few) motifs present in most sequences, the rest is random background, and no gaps in the motif. Applications beyond promoters include protein sorting signals, RNA binding sites and splice signals.

## Word Enrichment {#word-enrichment}

Enumerate all $k$-mers ($4^k$ for DNA), count them, and compare with the background expectation. For independent bases:

$$
P_0(w) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} q(w_i), \qquad \lambda = M P_0(w), \qquad \text{enrichment} = z / \lambda
$$

where $z$ is the observed count and $M = \sum_s \max(L_s - k + 1, 0)$ counts valid start positions. Enrichment is a ratio relative to the chosen background, not a significance test.

**Lecture example.** In 761 bases (A 31%, C 19%, G 23%, T 27%), GAGGAG has $p = 0.31^2 \cdot 0.234^4 \approx 0.00027$, so $\lambda \approx 0.20$; one observed occurrence gives an enrichment of about 4.9.

Real sequence is not i.i.d. CG is depleted in vertebrate genomes because methylated C deaminates to T: the independent model expects about 33.6 CG dinucleotides, while 24 are observed (enrichment ≈ 0.71). A first-order Markov background reproduces dinucleotide counts exactly, so CG enrichment becomes 1 by construction.

## Why a Markov Background Matters {#why-a-markov-background-matters}

Neighbouring bases are correlated, and an independent model can mistake composition biases for motifs. A first-order Markov model uses

$$
P_0(w) = f(w_1) \prod_{i=2}^{k} P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}).
$$

In the course example (761 bases, 144 C, 178 G, 24 CG, 30 GC, one observed CGCG), ignoring edge effects:

$$
P_0(\text{CGCG}) = \frac{144}{761} \cdot \frac{24}{144} \cdot \frac{30}{178} \cdot \frac{24}{144}
$$

::: table align="center" title="CGCG under two backgrounds"

| Background | Expected count $\lambda$ | Enrichment $1/\lambda$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Independent bases | 1.49 | 0.67 |
| First-order Markov | 0.674 | 1.48 |

:::

The same word moves from apparent depletion to enrichment purely because of the background: the null model defines what counts as surprising.

## Significance {#significance}

For rare, roughly independent occurrences, $K \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and

$$
p = P(K \ge z) = \sum_{j=z}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^j}{j!}.
$$

Enrichment measures effect size; the p-value measures surprise under the null. Self-overlapping or periodic words create dependent counts, and testing many words requires multiple-testing correction. Fisher's exact test compares word hits and non-hits between foreground and background in a 2×2 table, summing the hypergeometric tail; composition, lengths and counting units must be matched.

When the input is an expression experiment, a fixed cutoff is arbitrary. In a miR-21 knock-down, direct targets may rise in expression once repression is released, so their 3′ UTRs should be enriched for target sites. Ranking genes by expression change and testing enrichment across successive cutoffs avoids choosing one threshold; Sylamer uses Fisher statistics along the ranking and cWords a related approach. A sequence hit alone does not prove direct targeting.

When testing a single sequence, estimate the background **excluding** that sequence (lecture: 6 GC in a 42-bp sequence, $\lambda \approx 1.40$–1.65, $p \approx 0.003$–0.007). In the miR-21 knock-down, about 1200 of the 4096 six-mers had $p < 0.01$ in the up-regulated 3′ UTRs, which shows why multiple testing and careful statistics matter; the seed-complementary words stand out. A variable motif is split over many different enriched words, which motivates representing it with a matrix instead.

## Variable Motifs and Gibbs Sampling {#variable-motifs-and-gibbs-sampling}

Exact-word methods split a variable motif into many strings; a probability matrix combines them. With width $k$ and candidate start $a$:

$$
S(x, a) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \log_2 \frac{p_i(x_{a+i-1})}{q(x_{a+i-1})}, \qquad
p_i(b) = \frac{c_i(b) + \alpha(b)}{\sum_{b'} [c_i(b') + \alpha(b')]}
$$

Known sites would give the matrix, and a known matrix would give the sites. Gibbs sampling breaks this circularity by updating one sequence at a time, assuming a fixed width, one ungapped site per sequence, and independent positions:

::: steps
1. Choose a random motif start in every sequence.
2. Select one sequence $r$ and temporarily leave it out.
3. Estimate the motif matrix from the current sites in the remaining sequences, with pseudocounts.
4. Score every valid start in sequence $r$.
5. Convert the scores into probabilities and sample a new start.
6. Repeat, check stability, and use several random initialisations.
:::

Leaving $r$ out prevents its current site from reinforcing the matrix used to rescore it. With a fixed background and uniform prior over starts, $P(x \mid a, \Theta) = Q(x)\, 2^{S(x,a)}$, where the all-background term $Q(x)$ cancels on normalisation:

$$
P(a \mid x, \Theta) = \frac{2^{S(x,a)}}{\sum_j 2^{S(x,j)}}
$$

(With natural-log scores, use $e^{S}$.) The new start is sampled, not chosen as the maximum, so lower-scoring positions remain possible and the sampler can explore. For scores $(0, 1, 2)$ the weights are $(1, 2, 4)$ and the probabilities $(1/7, 2/7, 4/7)$; a uniform draw $u = 0.6$ falls in $[3/7, 1)$ and selects the third site.

## EM and MEME {#em-and-meme}

Instead of sampling one site per sequence, the **expectation–maximisation (EM)** algorithm uses soft assignments:

- **E-step:** for every sequence and every start position, compute the posterior probability that the motif starts there, $P(a \mid x, W) \propto 2^{S(x,a)}$.
- **M-step:** re-estimate the matrix from all windows, weighted by these posteriors (expected counts), plus pseudocounts.

Iterate until the likelihood stops increasing. EM is deterministic and climbs to a local maximum, so it needs many starting points. **MEME** seeds EM from the words in the data, supports one site per sequence (OOPS), zero or one (ZOOPS) or any number (TCM), and can report several motifs.

::: table align="center" title="Gibbs sampling, EM and Viterbi-style training"

| Method | Assignment of sites | Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Gibbs sampling | One sampled site per sequence (hard, stochastic) | Can escape local optima |
| EM / MEME | Posterior weights over all positions (soft) | Deterministic, local optimum |
| Viterbi-style training | Best site only (hard argmax) | Fast, greedy |

:::

Baum–Welch for HMMs ([Topic 8](/docs/ku-bsa/hidden-markov-models/)) is the same EM principle.

## Limitations and Related Methods {#limitations-and-related-methods}

Finite runs do not guarantee the best motif; the sampler can stay near local solutions or shifted versions of the motif. The width may be wrong, and sequences may contain zero or several sites. Multiple starts, joint shifts, width changes, flexible site counts and better backgrounds help, but a standard PSSM still cannot model dependencies between positions. The course application was the ribosome binding signal upstream of *E. coli* genes.

An HMM can represent background and successive motif-position states, with the hidden path giving motif locations. MEME uses expectation maximisation from candidate seeds, supports different occurrence models, and can find several motifs, again with possible local optima. An enriched motif is a candidate biological signal; enrichment alone does not prove binding or regulatory function.

### Full Script {#full-script}

::: details Full spoken script

My topic is motif discovery. The biological motivation: from an expression experiment we get a cluster of genes that are switched on or off together. A likely explanation is that they share a regulator, such as a transcription factor binding to their promoters. So we search their promoter sequences for a common element. In motif scanning, which is the previous topic, the motif is known and we look for its occurrences. In motif discovery, neither the motif nor its locations are known. This is hard because binding sites contain little information and genomes are heterogeneous. The usual simplifying assumptions are: one motif present in most sequences, the rest of each sequence is random background, and no gaps in the motif.

There are two main approaches: word-based methods, which count all possible words and compare with a background — exhaustive — and weight-matrix based methods, which try to estimate a PSSM that scores high in the sequences, by sampling — not exhaustive.

Word-based first. If letters are independent with frequencies $q(b)$, a word $x_1\ldots x_k$ has probability $p=\prod q(x_i)$, and in $l$ positions we expect $\lambda=p\cdot l$ occurrences. The enrichment is observed divided by expected.

$$
p=\prod_i q(x_i),\qquad \lambda=p\cdot l,\qquad \text{enrichment}=\frac{\text{observed}}{\lambda}
$$

In the lecture sequences of 761 bases, GAGGAG is expected about 0.2 times and seen once, an enrichment of about 5. But real DNA is not a sequence of independent letters: neighbouring bases are correlated. For example CG is depleted in vertebrates because methylated C deaminates to T. With an independent model, CG is expected 34 times but seen 24 times. So we use a Markov background, where each base depends on the previous $m$ bases. For a first-order model:

$$
P(CGCG)=f(C)\,P(G|C)\,P(C|G)\,P(G|C)=\frac{144}{761}\cdot\frac{24}{144}\cdot\frac{30}{178}\cdot\frac{24}{144}\approx0.00089
$$

With the independent background, CGCG looks depleted, enrichment 0.67; with the Markov background, which already knows that CG is rare, the same single observation is an enrichment of 1.48. So the background defines what we call surprising.

To judge significance, the count of a rare word is approximately Poisson with mean $\lambda$, and the p-value is the probability of seeing at least the observed count. This approximation breaks down for self-overlapping words like GGGGGGG, whose occurrences are dependent; and when testing all 4096 six-mers we must correct for multiple testing. When testing one sequence, the background should be estimated excluding that sequence.

Often we have two sets, for example up-regulated genes and the rest. Then we count word occurrences in both and test with a 2×2 table using the hypergeometric distribution — Fisher's exact test. In the miR-21 knock-down experiment, the 3′ UTRs of up-regulated genes were enriched for words complementary to the miRNA seed. Since any cutoff for "up-regulated" is arbitrary, Sylamer ranks all genes by expression change and computes the test for every cutoff.

The problem with words: a variable motif — like an acceptor site — is spread over many different words, so we get a large number of similar enriched words. It is more natural to search directly for a PSSM that captures the variation. But now we have a circular problem: if we knew the sites we could build the matrix, and if we knew the matrix we could find the sites.

Gibbs sampling solves this iteratively. Assumptions: one site per sequence, fixed width, independent motif positions, independent background.

First, choose a random start position in every sequence. Then pick one sequence and leave it out. Build a PSSM from the current sites in all the other sequences, with pseudocounts. Score every possible window in the left-out sequence, and convert the scores to probabilities: the probability that the motif starts at $a$ is proportional to $2^{S(a)}$, normalised over all positions — the background part cancels. Then, and this is essential, *sample* a new start from this distribution rather than taking the maximum. Repeat thousands of times, choosing a new sequence each time.

For example, if three windows have scores 0, 1 and 2, the weights are 1, 2 and 4, and the probabilities are 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7. The computer draws a uniform random number; if it is 0.6 it falls in the interval from 3/7 to 1, so we pick the third window. The lower-scoring windows still have a chance, which lets the algorithm escape local optima. Leaving out the sequence prevents its current site from voting for itself. Gradually, the sites align and the matrix becomes sharp: on 50-base upstream regions of 1200 *E. coli* genes, it finds the ribosome binding site.

Gibbs sampling is a heuristic: there is no guarantee of the global optimum. It can get stuck in a shifted version of the motif, and we must choose the width. Extensions include shift moves, changing the width, allowing zero or several sites per sequence, and a better background — and in practice, running from several random starts.

The deterministic relative is the EM algorithm, used in MEME: instead of sampling one position, the E-step computes for every position the posterior probability that the motif starts there, and the M-step updates the matrix with these probabilities as weighted counts. MEME seeds from all words, allows several sites per sequence and finds several motifs. This is the same principle as Baum–Welch for hidden Markov models, and indeed an HMM with background and motif states can be used for discovery. Finally, an enriched motif is a hypothesis: it still needs experimental support that a factor actually binds there.

:::

## Follow-up Questions {#follow-up-questions}

::: note How to use these questions

Questions marked ★ were raised in the revision sessions on 27 and 29 October. Answer directly first, then justify with the model, then name an assumption or limitation. Each answer is collapsed so the list can be used for self-testing.

:::

::: collapse

- ★ Explain Gibbs sampling.

  Iterative stochastic search for motif positions and PSSM simultaneously: random start sites → leave one sequence out → PSSM from the others (+ pseudocounts) → score every window of the left-out sequence → probabilities ∝ $2^S$ (normalised) → sample a new site → repeat many times until stable; multiple restarts. It is an MCMC sampler of the posterior over site configurations; heuristic, no guarantee of global optimum.

- ★ Why sample rather than take the best position? Why leave the sequence out?

  Sampling lets lower-scoring positions occasionally be chosen, so the algorithm can escape local optima (a deterministic greedy update would lock onto the first decent pattern). Leaving the sequence out prevents its current site — possibly random — from contributing to the matrix used to rescore it (self-reinforcement).

- ★ Explain the EM algorithm in this context.

  Expectation–maximisation for motif finding (MEME): E-step — given the current PSSM, compute for every sequence and every start position the posterior probability $Z_{ja}$ that the motif starts there ($\propto2^{S}$); M-step — re-estimate the PSSM from all windows weighted by $Z$ (expected counts) plus pseudocounts; iterate until the likelihood converges. Deterministic, climbs to a local maximum, so it is started from many seeds (MEME uses all words as seeds). Gibbs is the stochastic "hard assignment" counterpart; Baum–Welch is EM for HMMs.

- What does "on the computer, sampling" mean?

  Compute cumulative probabilities, draw $u\sim U[0,1)$, choose the interval containing $u$. Scores (0, 1, 2) → probabilities (1/7, 2/7, 4/7) → intervals [0, .14), [.14, .43), [.43, 1); $u=0.6$ → third.

- What problems does Gibbs sampling have and how to fix them?

  Converges to shifted patterns (motif offset by a few positions) → try shifting all sites left/right; unknown width → shrink/enlarge moves or run several widths; sequences with zero or multiple sites → ZOOPS/TCM models; local optima → many restarts; poor background → Markov background.

- Why a Markov background for words?

  Real sequences have dinucleotide (CpG depletion), codon and repeat structure. An independent background attributes these biases to motifs (false enrichments) or hides real ones. A Markov model of order $m$ conditions on the previous $m$ bases, so a word is enriched only beyond what local composition explains (CGCG: 0.67 → 1.48).

- How do you compute a p-value for a word count?

  Expected count $\lambda=l\cdot p(w)$; count ~ Poisson($\lambda$); p = $P(K\ge z)=1-\sum_{k<z}e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. Correct for multiple tests (4096 words). Invalid for self-overlapping words.

- How do you compare a foreground set to a background set?

  2×2 table (word present/absent × foreground/background); hypergeometric probability of seeing ≥ k in the foreground given margins → Fisher's exact test. Sylamer repeats this along a ranked gene list.

- Why don't word-based methods work well for variable motifs?

  One motif corresponds to many exact words each with modest counts; enrichment is split among them, many redundant words appear, and the true motif must be reassembled. A PSSM represents variation directly.

- What are the assumptions of the Gibbs model?

  One site per sequence, fixed width, ungapped, positions independent, background i.i.d.; sequences independent.

- How would an HMM be used for motif discovery?

  Background state with self-loop → motif states $M_1..M_n$ (position-specific emissions) → background; train with Baum–Welch on the unaligned sequences; the Viterbi/posterior path marks motif occurrences. Risk of local maxima.

- Real example from the lecture?

  miR-21 knock-down: up-regulated genes' 3′UTRs enriched for 6-mers complementary to the miRNA seed (1200 of 4096 words p < 0.01 → need careful statistics); Gibbs on *E. coli* upstream regions finds the Shine–Dalgarno RBS.

:::
