## Vector matrix calculations, pen and paper {#vector-matrix-calculations}

### Problem \#1 {#problem-1-1}

Let $\mathbf{v}$ be the vector $[-2,b,7,2,a]^T$. Compute $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^T$.

### Solution \#1 {#solution-1-1}

$\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}$ is a scalar value computed as follows:

$$
\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v} = (-2)^2 + b^2 + 7^2 + 2^2 + a^2 = 4 + b^2 + 49 + 4 + a^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 57
$$

$\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^T$ is a matrix computed as follows:

$$
\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^T = \begin{bmatrix}
-2 \\
b \\
7 \\
2 \\
a
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
-2 & b & 7 & 2 & a
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
4 & -2b & -14 & -4 & -2a \\
-2b & b^2 & 7b & 2b & ab \\
-14 & 7b & 49 & 14 & 7a \\
-4 & 2b & 14 & 4 & 2a \\
-2a & ab & 7a & 2a & a^2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Problem \#2 {#problem-1-2}

Let $A$ and $B$ the following matrices:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
-5 & 4 & -6 \\
3 & 4 & -6 \\
8 & -6 & 8
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
B = \begin{bmatrix}
-4 & 8 \\
-5 & -9 \\
4 & -7
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Compute $\mathbf{v} = B.[x,y]^T$, $\mathbf{w} = A.\mathbf{v}$. Then compute $C = A.B$ and $\mathbf{z} = C.[x,y]^T$.

### Solution \#2 {#solution-1-2}

First, multiply each row of $B$ by the column vector $[x,y]^T$:

$$
\mathbf{v} = B.[x,y]^T = \begin{bmatrix}
-4 & 8 \\
-5 & -9 \\
4 & -7
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
x \\
y
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
-4x + 8y \\
-5x - 9y \\
4x - 7y
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, multiply $A$ by $\mathbf{v}$. Each component $w_i$ is obtained by taking the dot product of the $i$ th row of $A$ with $\mathbf{v}$ and then collecting the coefficients of $x$ and $y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{w}
&= A.\mathbf{v} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
-5 & 4 & -6 \\
3 & 4 & -6 \\
8 & -6 & 8
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
-4x + 8y \\
-5x - 9y \\
4x - 7y
\end{bmatrix}, \\
w_1
&= (-5)(-4x + 8y) + 4(-5x - 9y) - 6(4x - 7y) \\
&= -24x - 34y, \\
w_2
&= 3(-4x + 8y) + 4(-5x - 9y) - 6(4x - 7y) \\
&= -56x + 30y, \\
w_3
&= 8(-4x + 8y) - 6(-5x - 9y) + 8(4x - 7y) \\
&= 30x + 62y, \\
\mathbf{w}
&= \begin{bmatrix}
-24x - 34y \\
-56x + 30y \\
30x + 62y
\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now compute the product $C = A.B$ directly. Since $A$ is $3 \times 3$ and $B$ is $3 \times 2$, the product $C$ is a $3 \times 2$ matrix. Each entry $c_{ij}$ is the dot product of the $i$ th row of $A$ with the $j$ th column of $B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C
&= A.B
= \begin{bmatrix}
-5 & 4 & -6 \\
3 & 4 & -6 \\
8 & -6 & 8
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
-4 & 8 \\
-5 & -9 \\
4 & -7
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
(-5)(-4) + 4(-5) + (-6)(4) & (-5)(8) + 4(-9) + (-6)(-7) \\
3(-4) + 4(-5) + (-6)(4) & 3(8) + 4(-9) + (-6)(-7) \\
8(-4) + (-6)(-5) + 8(4) & 8(8) + (-6)(-9) + 8(-7)
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
20 - 20 - 24 & -40 - 36 + 42 \\
-12 - 20 - 24 & 24 - 36 + 42 \\
-32 + 30 + 32 & 64 + 54 - 56
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
-24 & -34 \\
-56 & 30 \\
30 & 62
\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, apply $C$ to $[x,y]^T$:

$$
\mathbf{z} = C.[x,y]^T = \begin{bmatrix}
-24 & -34 \\
-56 & 30 \\
30 & 62
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x \\
y
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
-24x - 34y \\
-56x + 30y \\
30x + 62y
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The result satisfies $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{w}$, as expected from the associativity of matrix multiplication: $\mathbf{w} = A.(B.[x,y]^T) = (A.B).[x,y]^T = C.[x,y]^T = \mathbf{z}$.

### Problem \#3 {#problem-1-3}

Let $A$ be the matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & -1 \\
8 - x & x - 6 & x - 6 \\
x - 7 & 5 - x & 5 - x
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Compute respectively $A^2 = A.A$ and $A^3 = A.A.A$. Such a matrix $A$ is called _nilpotent_.

### Solution \#3 {#solution-1-3}

Each entry of $A^2$ is the dot product of a row of $A$ with a column of $A$. The second and third columns of $A$ are identical, so the second and third columns of $A^2$ are identical as well, and only the first two columns need to be computed.

For the first row of $A^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A^2)_{11} &= 1 - (8 - x) - (x - 7) = 0, \\
(A^2)_{12} &= -1 - (x - 6) - (5 - x) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

For the second row, factoring out $(x - 6)$ removes all terms in $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A^2)_{21} &= (8 - x) + (x - 6)(8 - x) + (x - 6)(x - 7) \\
&= (8 - x) + (x - 6)\big[(8 - x) + (x - 7)\big] \\
&= (8 - x) + (x - 6) = 2, \\
(A^2)_{22} &= -(8 - x) + (x - 6)(x - 6) + (x - 6)(5 - x) \\
&= (x - 8) + (x - 6)\big[(x - 6) + (5 - x)\big] \\
&= (x - 8) - (x - 6) = -2
\end{aligned}
$$

For the third row, factoring out $(5 - x)$ works in the same way:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A^2)_{31} &= (x - 7) + (5 - x)(8 - x) + (5 - x)(x - 7) \\
&= (x - 7) + (5 - x)\big[(8 - x) + (x - 7)\big] \\
&= (x - 7) + (5 - x) = -2, \\
(A^2)_{32} &= -(x - 7) + (5 - x)(x - 6) + (5 - x)(5 - x) \\
&= (7 - x) + (5 - x)\big[(x - 6) + (5 - x)\big] \\
&= (7 - x) - (5 - x) = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $A^2$ no longer depends on $x$:

$$
A^2 = A.A = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0 \\
2 & -2 & -2 \\
-2 & 2 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, compute $A^3 = A^2.A$. The first row of $A^2$ is zero, so the first row of $A^3$ is zero. For the second row:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A^3)_{21} &= 2 - 2(8 - x) - 2(x - 7) = 2 - 16 + 14 = 0, \\
(A^3)_{22} &= -2 - 2(x - 6) - 2(5 - x) = -2 + 12 - 10 = 0, \\
(A^3)_{23} &= (A^3)_{22} = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The third row of $A^2$ is the negative of its second row, so the third row of $A^3$ is also zero. Hence

$$
A^3 = A.A.A = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$A^2 \neq 0$ but $A^3 = 0$ for every value of $x$, which is why $A$ is called nilpotent.

### Problem \#4 {#problem-1-4}

Let $A$ be the matrix

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
-95 & 72 & -4 \\
-144 & 109 & -6 \\
76 & -57 & 3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Let $\mathbf{e}_1 = [1,0,0]^T$, $\mathbf{e}_2 = [0,1,0]^T$ and $\mathbf{e}_3 = [0,0,1]^T$. Compute respectively the solutions $\mathbf{f}_1$, $\mathbf{f}_2$ and $\mathbf{f}_3$ of the linear systems

$$
A.\mathbf{f}_1 = \mathbf{e}_1, \quad A.\mathbf{f}_2 = \mathbf{e}_2, \quad A.\mathbf{f}_3 = \mathbf{e}_3
$$

Let $B$ be the matrix whose columns are $\mathbf{f}_1$, $\mathbf{f}_2$ and $\mathbf{f}_3$. Then compute $A.B$ and $B.A$. What does it mean?

### Solution \#4 {#solution-1-4}

The three systems share the same coefficient matrix and differ only in their right-hand sides, so they can be solved together with Cramer's rule. Let $C_{ij}$ denote the cofactor of the entry $a_{ij}$, that is, $(-1)^{i+j}$ times the determinant of the $2 \times 2$ matrix left after deleting row $i$ and column $j$ of $A$. Because the right-hand side $\mathbf{e}_j$ has a single non-zero entry, Cramer's rule reduces to

$$
(\mathbf{f}_j)_i = \frac{C_{ji}}{\det A}
$$

The nine cofactors are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{11} &= (109)(3) - (-6)(-57) = 327 - 342 = -15, \\
C_{12} &= -\big[(-144)(3) - (-6)(76)\big] = -(-432 + 456) = -24, \\
C_{13} &= (-144)(-57) - (109)(76) = 8208 - 8284 = -76, \\
C_{21} &= -\big[(72)(3) - (-4)(-57)\big] = -(216 - 228) = 12, \\
C_{22} &= (-95)(3) - (-4)(76) = -285 + 304 = 19, \\
C_{23} &= -\big[(-95)(-57) - (72)(76)\big] = -(5415 - 5472) = 57, \\
C_{31} &= (72)(-6) - (-4)(109) = -432 + 436 = 4, \\
C_{32} &= -\big[(-95)(-6) - (-4)(-144)\big] = -(570 - 576) = 6, \\
C_{33} &= (-95)(109) - (72)(-144) = -10355 + 10368 = 13
\end{aligned}
$$

Expanding the determinant along the first row gives

$$
\det A = a_{11}C_{11} + a_{12}C_{12} + a_{13}C_{13} = (-95)(-15) + (72)(-24) + (-4)(-76) = 1425 - 1728 + 304 = 1
$$

Since $\det A = 1 \neq 0$, each system has a unique solution, and $\mathbf{f}_j$ is the $j$ th row of cofactors written as a column:

$$
\mathbf{f}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}
-15 \\
-24 \\
-76
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{f}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}
12 \\
19 \\
57
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{f}_3 = \begin{bmatrix}
4 \\
6 \\
13
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Placing the three solutions as columns gives

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix}
-15 & 12 & 4 \\
-24 & 19 & 6 \\
-76 & 57 & 13
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The $j$ th column of $A.B$ is $A.\mathbf{f}_j = \mathbf{e}_j$, so $A.B$ is the identity matrix. Written out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A.B
&= \begin{bmatrix}
-95 & 72 & -4 \\
-144 & 109 & -6 \\
76 & -57 & 3
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
-15 & 12 & 4 \\
-24 & 19 & 6 \\
-76 & 57 & 13
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
1425 - 1728 + 304 & -1140 + 1368 - 228 & -380 + 432 - 52 \\
2160 - 2616 + 456 & -1728 + 2071 - 342 & -576 + 654 - 78 \\
-1140 + 1368 - 228 & 912 - 1083 + 171 & 304 - 342 + 39
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The product in the other order has to be computed separately, since matrix multiplication is not commutative in general:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B.A
&= \begin{bmatrix}
-15 & 12 & 4 \\
-24 & 19 & 6 \\
-76 & 57 & 13
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
-95 & 72 & -4 \\
-144 & 109 & -6 \\
76 & -57 & 3
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
1425 - 1728 + 304 & -1080 + 1308 - 228 & 60 - 72 + 12 \\
2280 - 2736 + 456 & -1728 + 2071 - 342 & 96 - 114 + 18 \\
7220 - 8208 + 988 & -5472 + 6213 - 741 & 304 - 342 + 39
\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both products equal the identity matrix, $A.B = B.A = I$, which means that ==$B$ is the inverse of $A$==, i.e. $B = A^{-1}$ (and $A = B^{-1}$).

### Problem \#5 {#problem-1-5}

**\[Optional\]** Let $A$ and $B$ be square matrices of size $(n, n)$. Show that $(A.B)^T = B^T.A^T$.

### Solution \#5 {#solution-1-5}

Write $a_{ij}$ and $b_{ij}$ for the entries of $A$ and $B$ in row $i$ and column $j$. The transpose swaps the row and column indices, so $(M^T)_{ij} = M_{ji}$ for any matrix $M$. The product is defined entrywise by

$$
(A.B)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}
$$

It suffices to compare the $(i,j)$ entries of both sides for arbitrary $1 \le i, j \le n$. For the left-hand side:

$$
\big((A.B)^T\big)_{ij} = (A.B)_{ji} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{jk} b_{ki}
$$

For the right-hand side:

$$
\big(B^T.A^T\big)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (B^T)_{ik} (A^T)_{kj} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} b_{ki} a_{jk} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{jk} b_{ki}
$$

The last step only reorders the two scalar factors in each term, which is allowed because multiplication of numbers is commutative. Both sides have the same $(i,j)$ entry for every $i$ and $j$, hence

$$
(A.B)^T = B^T.A^T
$$

## Vector matrix calculations, Python {#vector-matrix-calculations-python}

### Problem \#1 {#problem-2-1}

An inner and an outer product. Compute the inner product of `a = np.array([17,6,-5.5,3])` and `b = np.array([-4.9,1.17,0,2])`.

Use `np.dot()` or, preferably, the infix notation `@`. Computing the outer product requires a bit of extra work. Display the _shape_ of `a`, `b` and `b.T` (`b.shape` and `b.T.shape`). To force Python-numpy to distinguish between column and row vectors, 1D arrays can be reshaped: Try this piece of code interactively:

```python
print(a@b)
a.shape = (4,1) # a matrix with 1 column (a column vector)
b.shape = (4,1) # a matrix with 1 column (a column vector)
print(a)
print(b)
print(b.shape)
print(b.T.shape)
print(a@b)
print(a.T@b)
print(float(a.T@b))
print(a@b.T)
```

What do you get?

### Solution \#1 {#solution-2-1}

The outputs below were obtained with NumPy 2.5.5. Before reshaping, `a` and `b` are 1D arrays, which are neither row nor column vectors:

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([17, 6, -5.5, 3])
b = np.array([-4.9, 1.17, 0, 2])

print(a.shape, b.shape, b.T.shape)
print(a @ b)
print(np.dot(a, b))
```

```text
(4,) (4,) (4,)
-70.28000000000002
-70.28000000000002
```

Transposing a 1D array has no effect, so `b.T.shape` is still `(4,)`. For two 1D arrays, `@` and `np.dot()` both return the inner product as a scalar:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}
  &= (17)(-4.9) + (6)(1.17) + (-5.5)(0) + (3)(2) \\
  &= -83.3 + 7.02 + 0 + 6 \\
  &= -70.28
\end{aligned}
$$

After setting both shapes to `(4,1)`, `a` and `b` become 2D arrays with one column, and the transpose now changes the shape:

```python
a.shape = (4, 1)
b.shape = (4, 1)
print(a)
print(b)
print(b.shape)
print(b.T.shape)
```

```text
[[17. ]
 [ 6. ]
 [-5.5]
 [ 3. ]]
[[-4.9 ]
 [ 1.17]
 [ 0.  ]
 [ 2.  ]]
(4, 1)
(1, 4)
```

With 2D arrays, `@` follows the rules of matrix multiplication, so the three remaining products behave differently:

| Expression | Shapes               | Result                                                              |
| ---------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `a @ b`    | $(4,1) \times (4,1)$ | `ValueError`, because the inner dimensions $1$ and $4$ do not match |
| `a.T @ b`  | $(1,4) \times (4,1)$ | A $(1,1)$ matrix holding the inner product                          |
| `a @ b.T`  | $(4,1) \times (1,4)$ | A $(4,4)$ matrix, the outer product                                 |

`print(a@b)` stops the script with this `ValueError`, so the remaining lines have to be run separately. `print(a.T@b)` and `print(a@b.T)` give:

```text
[[-70.28]]
[[-83.3    19.89    0.     34.   ]
 [-29.4     7.02    0.     12.   ]
 [ 26.95   -6.435   0.    -11.   ]
 [-14.7     3.51    0.      6.   ]]
```

::: warning Behavior in NumPy 2.5
Two lines of the exercise behave differently in this NumPy version than the exercise text suggests:

- `print(float(a.T@b))` is meant to convert the $1 \times 1$ matrix to a Python scalar, but it raises `TypeError: only 0-dimensional arrays can be converted to Python scalars`. Extract the single element explicitly instead, for example with `(a.T @ b).item()` or `float((a.T @ b)[0, 0])`.
- Assigning to `a.shape` still works but emits a `DeprecationWarning`. The recommended alternative is `a = a.reshape(4, 1)`.
:::

### Problem \#2 {#problem-2-2}

Let $\mathbf{t} = [-3,2,11]^T$. Compute the cross-product $\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{t}$ of $\mathbf{t}$ and itself. (`numpy` has a cross-product operation, feel free to implement your own). Let $\mathbf{a} = [-2,6,1]^T$. Compute $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{t}$. Then compute the inner products $\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}$.

### Solution \#2 {#solution-2-2}

The cross product of two 3D vectors is defined componentwise by

$$
\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix}
t_2 a_3 - t_3 a_2 \\
t_3 a_1 - t_1 a_3 \\
t_1 a_2 - t_2 a_1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

`np.cross()` implements this formula:

```python
import numpy as np

t = np.array([-3, 2, 11])
a = np.array([-2, 6, 1])

b = np.cross(t, a)
c = np.cross(a, t)

print(np.cross(t, t))
print(b)
print(c)
print(t @ b)
print(a @ b)
```

```text
[0 0 0]
[-64 -19 -14]
[64 19 14]
0
0
```

So $\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{b} = [-64,-19,-14]^T$, $\mathbf{c} = [64,19,14]^T = -\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = 0$.

The cross product of a vector with itself is $\mathbf{0}$. The cross product is anti-commutative, $\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{t} = -\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{a}$, so $\mathbf{c} = -\mathbf{b}$. The vector $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{a}$ is perpendicular to both $\mathbf{t}$ and $\mathbf{a}$, so both inner products are $0$.

### Problem \#3 {#problem-2-3}

To a 3D vector $\mathbf{t}$, one associates the matrix

$$
\hat{\mathbf{t}} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -t_3 & t_2 \\
t_3 & 0 & -t_1 \\
-t_2 & t_1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Write a function that takes a 3D vector $\mathbf{t}$ and associates the corresponding matrix $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$. With $\mathbf{t}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ from the previous question, compute $\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{t}$ (matrix vector product) as well as $\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{a}$. What do you get?

### Solution \#3 {#solution-2-3}

NumPy indices start at 0, so $t_1$, $t_2$ and $t_3$ are `t[0]`, `t[1]` and `t[2]`:

```python
def hat(t):
    return np.array([
        [0, -t[2], t[1]],
        [t[2], 0, -t[0]],
        [-t[1], t[0], 0],
    ])

t_hat = hat(t)
print(t_hat)
print(t_hat @ t)
print(t_hat @ a)
```

```text
[[  0 -11   2]
 [ 11   0   3]
 [ -2  -3   0]]
[0 0 0]
[-64 -19 -14]
```

$\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{t}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{a} = [-64,-19,-14]^T = \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{a}$.

### Problem \#4 {#problem-2-4}

For a 3D vector $\mathbf{v}$, the operation $\mathbf{v} \mapsto \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{v}$ is _linear_. So it can be represented by a matrix. Which one?

### Solution \#4 {#solution-2-4}

The matrix is $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$. Multiplying $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ by an arbitrary $\mathbf{v} = [v_1,v_2,v_3]^T$ gives

$$
\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -t_3 & t_2 \\
t_3 & 0 & -t_1 \\
-t_2 & t_1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
v_1 \\
v_2 \\
v_3
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
t_2 v_3 - t_3 v_2 \\
t_3 v_1 - t_1 v_3 \\
t_1 v_2 - t_2 v_1
\end{bmatrix}
= \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{v}
$$

which is the componentwise definition of the cross product from [Solution #2](#solution-2-2). Hence $\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{v} = \hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{v}$ for every $\mathbf{v}$.

### Problem \#5 {#problem-2-5}

Check that $\hat{\mathbf{t}}^T = -\hat{\mathbf{t}}$. Compute $\hat{\mathbf{t}}^2 = \hat{\mathbf{t}}.\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ (matrix-matrix product). What is the transpose of $\hat{\mathbf{t}}^2$? What about $\hat{\mathbf{t}}^3 = \hat{\mathbf{t}}.\hat{\mathbf{t}}.\hat{\mathbf{t}}$? You may want first to perform numerical computations in Python!

### Solution \#5 {#solution-2-5}

The numerical computations with $\mathbf{t} = [-3,2,11]^T$:

```python
print(np.array_equal(t_hat.T, -t_hat))

t_hat2 = t_hat @ t_hat
print(t_hat2)
print(np.array_equal(t_hat2.T, t_hat2))

t_hat3 = t_hat2 @ t_hat
print(t_hat3)
print(t @ t)
```

```text
True
[[-125   -6  -33]
 [  -6 -130   22]
 [ -33   22  -13]]
True
[[    0  1474  -268]
 [-1474     0  -402]
 [  268   402     0]]
134
```

$\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ is **skew-symmetric**: transposing it swaps each $t_i$ with $-t_i$ across the diagonal, and the diagonal is zero, so $\hat{\mathbf{t}}^T = -\hat{\mathbf{t}}$. Its square is symmetric, because

$$
(\hat{\mathbf{t}}^2)^T = \hat{\mathbf{t}}^T\hat{\mathbf{t}}^T = (-\hat{\mathbf{t}})(-\hat{\mathbf{t}}) = \hat{\mathbf{t}}^2
$$

where the first step uses $(A.B)^T = B^T.A^T$ from [Problem #5](#problem-1-5) of the first part.

$\hat{\mathbf{t}}^3$ is skew-symmetric again, and each of its entries is $-134 = -\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t}$ times the corresponding entry of $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ (for example $-134 \times (-11) = 1474$). This holds for every $\mathbf{t}$. Expanding the product gives $\hat{\mathbf{t}}^2 = \mathbf{t}\mathbf{t}^T - (\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t})I$, and $\mathbf{t}^T\hat{\mathbf{t}} = -(\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{t})^T = \mathbf{0}^T$ because $\hat{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{0}$. Hence

$$
\hat{\mathbf{t}}^3 = \hat{\mathbf{t}}^2\hat{\mathbf{t}} = \mathbf{t}(\mathbf{t}^T\hat{\mathbf{t}}) - (\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t})\,\hat{\mathbf{t}} = -(\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t})\,\hat{\mathbf{t}}
$$

## Derivatives {#derivatives}

Functions of one variable.

### Problem \#1 {#problem-3-1}

Compute the derivative $f'(x)$ of $f(x) = e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}$. (Hint: use the chain rule for the exponential, see the tables below.)

### Solution \#1 {#solution-3-1}

Write $f(x) = e^{u(x)}$ with $u(x) = -\frac{x^2}{2}$, so that $u'(x) = -x$. The chain rule for the exponential, $\left(e^{u}\right)' = u' e^{u}$, gives

$$
f'(x) = u'(x)\, e^{u(x)} = -x\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}
$$

### Problem \#2 {#problem-3-2}

Compute its second derivative, $f''(x)$. (Hint: reuse the previous rule and Leibniz rule).

### Solution \#2 {#solution-3-2}

$f'(x)$ is the product of $-x$ and $e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}$. The Leibniz rule $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$, together with the derivative of the exponential from [Solution #1](#solution-3-1), gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
f''(x)
&= (-x)'\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} + (-x) \left(e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}\right)' \\
&= -e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} + (-x)(-x)\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} \\
&= (x^2 - 1)\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Problem \#4 {#problem-3-4}

Compute the derivative $g'(t)$ of $g(t) = \cos(t)/\sin(t)$.

### Solution \#4 {#solution-3-4}

The quotient rule $\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$ with $u = \cos(t)$, $u' = -\sin(t)$, $v = \sin(t)$ and $v' = \cos(t)$ gives

$$
g'(t) = \frac{-\sin(t)\sin(t) - \cos(t)\cos(t)}{\sin^2(t)} = -\frac{\sin^2(t) + \cos^2(t)}{\sin^2(t)} = -\frac{1}{\sin^2(t)}
$$

The result holds wherever $g$ is defined, that is, for $\sin(t) \neq 0$, or $t \neq k\pi$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Equivalently, $g'(t) = -\csc^2(t) = -\left(1 + \cot^2(t)\right)$.

### Problem \#5 {#problem-3-5}

Let $f(x,t)$ be the Gaussian distribution function of variance $t$,

$$
f(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}}
$$

### Problem \#6 {#problem-3-6}

Compute $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)$, the first partial derivative of $f(x,t)$ with respect to $x$ (remember that you have to assume that $t$ is fixed and you use the rules of derivation for a function of one variable). It is also called the $x$-derivative of $f(x,t)$.

### Solution \#6 {#solution-3-6}

With $t$ fixed, the factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}$ is a constant, and the exponent $-\frac{x^2}{2t}$ has $x$-derivative $-\frac{x}{t}$. The chain rule for the exponential gives

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}} \left(-\frac{x}{t}\right) e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}} = -\frac{x}{t}\, f(x,t)
$$

### Problem \#7 {#problem-3-7}

Compute $\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)$, the $t$-derivative of $f(x,t)$.

### Solution \#7 {#solution-3-7}

With $x$ fixed, $f$ is the product of two functions of $t$, namely $(2\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}$ and $e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}}$. Their $t$-derivatives are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t} (2\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}} &= -\frac{1}{2}\,(2\pi)\,(2\pi t)^{-\frac{3}{2}} = -\frac{1}{2t}\,(2\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}, \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}} &= \frac{x^2}{2t^2}\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second line uses $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(-\frac{x^2}{2t}\right) = \frac{x^2}{2t^2}$. The Leibniz rule then gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)
&= -\frac{1}{2t}\,(2\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}} + (2\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\, \frac{x^2}{2t^2}\, e^{-\frac{x^2}{2t}} \\
&= \left(\frac{x^2}{2t^2} - \frac{1}{2t}\right) f(x,t) \\
&= \frac{x^2 - t}{2t^2}\, f(x,t)
\end{aligned}
$$

### Problem \#8 {#problem-3-8}

Compute $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(x,t)$, the second partial derivative of $f$ with respect to $x$, this is the $x$-derivative of $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)$.

### Solution \#8 {#solution-3-8}

From [Solution #6](#solution-3-6), $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -\frac{x}{t} f$ is the product of $-\frac{x}{t}$ and $f$. The Leibniz rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(x,t)
&= -\frac{1}{t}\, f(x,t) + \left(-\frac{x}{t}\right) \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t) \\
&= -\frac{1}{t}\, f(x,t) + \frac{x^2}{t^2}\, f(x,t) \\
&= \frac{x^2 - t}{t^2}\, f(x,t)
\end{aligned}
$$

### Problem \#9 {#problem-3-9}

Then show that

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(x,t)
$$

This is an extremely important property of this function, especially for many computer vision applications, where $t$ will play the role of a _spatial scale parameter_.

### Solution \#9 {#solution-3-9}

Comparing the results of [Solution #7](#solution-3-7) and [Solution #8](#solution-3-8):

$$
\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(x,t) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{x^2 - t}{t^2}\, f(x,t) = \frac{x^2 - t}{2t^2}\, f(x,t) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)
$$

so ==$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$== for every $x$ and every $t > 0$.

### Problem \#10 {#problem-3-10}

Plot these functions for $t = 1, 2, 4$, with $x \in [-15,15]$.

### Solution \#10 {#solution-3-10}

"These functions" is read here as $f$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}$ and $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$. Each one gets its own panel, with one curve per value of $t$:

```python
def gaussian(x, t):
    return np.exp(-(x**2) / (2 * t)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * t)

def gaussian_dx(x, t):
    return -(x / t) * gaussian(x, t)

def gaussian_dt(x, t):
    return (x**2 - t) / (2 * t**2) * gaussian(x, t)

def gaussian_dxx(x, t):
    return (x**2 - t) / t**2 * gaussian(x, t)

x = np.linspace(-15, 15, 1001)
panels = (gaussian, gaussian_dx, gaussian_dt, gaussian_dxx)
```
